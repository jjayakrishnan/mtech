1. Why Logic Fails with Uncertainty

Logic is crisp: a statement is true or false, always. But the real world is messy. Traffic jams, sensor noise, incomplete observations — none of these fit neatly into true/false boxes.

Everyday picture — the airport drive

You can reach Bangalore Airport from MG Road in 90 minutes via Route A — most of the time. What logic would you write? "IF no traffic AND no accident AND no roadblock THEN 90 minutes." You cannot enumerate every exception. Probability captures this naturally: "There is a 0.7 probability of arriving on time via Route A."

The table below contrasts the two paradigms. Notice that as you move right, the world becomes more realistic — and more uncertain.

PropertyLogic (Monotonic)Probability (Non-Monotonic)
ConsistencyComplete, consistentRelaxed — degrees of belief
KnowledgeComplete world assumedIncomplete, partial
WorldStatic, discreteDynamic, continuous
ModelPredicate logicProbabilistic models
Core idea

A prior probability \( P(A) \) is your belief before seeing evidence. A posterior probability \( P(A \mid E) \) is your updated belief after seeing evidence \( E \). Probability is the formal language for rational belief revision.

2. Probability Basics

Sample space and events

A sample space \( S \) is the set of all possible outcomes. For a die, \( S = \{1,2,3,4,5,6\} \). Each element \( x \in S \) is an atomic event. An event \( A \) is any subset of \( S \), e.g., "die roll < 4".

Axioms
  • \( 0 \leq P(A) \leq 1 \) for any event \( A \)
  • \( P(\text{true}) = 1 \), \( P(\text{false}) = 0 \)
  • \( P(A \lor B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \land B) \)

Conditional probability

Conditional probability asks: if I already know \( B \) happened, how likely is \( A \)?

$$ P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \land B)}{P(B)} $$

For example: \( P(\text{cavity} \mid \text{toothache}) = 0.8 \) means given a toothache, there is an 80% chance of a cavity.

Chain rule (product rule)

Rearranging the conditional probability definition gives:

$$ P(A \land B) = P(A \mid B) \cdot P(B) = P(B \mid A) \cdot P(A) $$

For three variables: \( P(A,B,C) = P(A \mid B,C) \cdot P(B \mid C) \cdot P(C) \).

Joint probability table — the dentist example

Two Boolean variables — Cavity and Toothache — give four possible combinations. Every cell holds the probability of that exact combination. All cells must sum to 1.

Exam trap — size explosion

A joint table for \( n \) Boolean variables needs \( 2^n \) entries. For 30 variables that is over a billion entries. This is why we need Bayesian Networks — they factorize the joint distribution compactly.

3. Bayes' Theorem

Bayes' theorem is the engine of probabilistic AI. It lets you flip a conditional probability: if you know \( P(\text{evidence} \mid \text{cause}) \), you can compute \( P(\text{cause} \mid \text{evidence}) \).

Why this matters — the rare disease problem

A disease affects 1 in 10,000 people. A test has 99% accuracy. You test positive. What is the chance you actually have the disease? Your gut says "99%". Bayes says much lower — because false positives on a huge healthy population overwhelm the true positives. The interactive below shows exactly this.

Derivation from scratch

Start from the definition of conditional probability twice:

$$ P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \land B)}{P(B)} \quad\text{and}\quad P(B \mid A) = \frac{P(A \land B)}{P(A)} $$

Both share \( P(A \land B) \). Set them equal after rearranging:

$$ P(A \land B) = P(A \mid B) \cdot P(B) = P(B \mid A) \cdot P(A) $$

Divide both sides by \( P(B) \) to get Bayes' theorem:

$$ \boxed{ P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(B \mid A) \cdot P(A)}{P(B)} } $$

Each term has a name:

  • \( P(A \mid B) \) — the posterior: what you want
  • \( P(B \mid A) \) — the likelihood: how likely is the evidence given the cause
  • \( P(A) \) — the prior: your belief before evidence
  • \( P(B) \) — the normalizer: makes everything sum to 1; often written as \( \alpha \)

Worked example — dentist

Step-by-step: P(cavity | toothache)

Given: \( P(\text{toothache} \mid \text{cavity}) = 0.6 \), \( P(\text{cavity}) = 0.2 \), \( P(\text{toothache}) = 0.2 \).

Step 1: Write Bayes' theorem: \( P(\text{cavity} \mid \text{toothache}) = \dfrac{P(\text{toothache} \mid \text{cavity}) \cdot P(\text{cavity})}{P(\text{toothache})} \)

Step 2: Plug in numbers: \( = \dfrac{0.6 \times 0.2}{0.2} = \dfrac{0.12}{0.2} = 0.6 \)

Interpretation: Even though the base rate of cavity is only 20%, knowing there is a toothache raises our belief to 60%.

Common exam mistake

Students often confuse \( P(A \mid B) \) with \( P(B \mid A) \). They are not the same. "The probability a patient has cancer given a positive test" \( \neq \) "the probability a positive test given cancer." The first is what the patient cares about; the second is what the lab measures. Bayes' theorem converts between them.

4. Independence and Conditional Independence

Two variables are independent if knowing one tells you nothing about the other:

$$ P(A \land B) = P(A) \cdot P(B) \quad \Leftrightarrow \quad P(A \mid B) = P(A) $$

Example: Weather and Toothache are independent. Rain does not affect whether your tooth hurts.

Conditional independence — the big one

Variables \( A \) and \( B \) are conditionally independent given \( C \) if:

$$ P(A, B \mid C) = P(A \mid C) \cdot P(B \mid C) $$

Example: Toothache and the dental probe Catching are conditionally independent given Cavity. Once you know there is a cavity, knowing about the toothache adds nothing to your prediction of the catch — and vice versa. The cavity explains everything.

Why this is huge

Suppose you have 10 symptoms and 1 disease. Without conditional independence the joint table needs \( 2^{11} = 2048 \) entries. With conditional independence (all symptoms independent given the disease) you only need \( 10 \times 2 + 2 = 22 \) entries. Bayesian Networks exploit exactly this structure.

Exam trap — D-separation

In a Bayesian Network, a node is conditionally independent of all non-descendants given its parents. A node is conditionally independent of all other nodes given its parents, children, and children's parents (the Markov blanket). D-separation is the graph test for this — follow the graph rules: serial chains, diverging forks, and converging forks each have different blocking conditions.

5. Bayesian Networks — Structure and CPTs

A Bayesian Network is a directed acyclic graph (DAG) where each node is a random variable and each edge means "directly influences." Attached to each node is a conditional probability table (CPT) giving \( P(X_i \mid \text{Parents}(X_i)) \).

What the graph tells you
  • An edge A → B means A is a direct cause of B.
  • No edge between A and B means they are independent (possibly conditional).
  • The joint distribution factors as: \( P(X_1,\ldots,X_n) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} P(X_i \mid \text{Parents}(X_i)) \)

Example: Weather / Cavity / Toothache / Catch

Four variables: Weather (W), Cavity (V), Toothache (T), Catch (C). Cavity causes both Toothache and Catch. Weather is independent of everything.

The CPTs use these values:

  • \( P(W = \text{good}) = 0.75 \) — Weather has no parents
  • \( P(V = \text{cavity}) = 0.2 \) — Cavity has no parents
  • \( P(T \mid V) = 0.6 \), \( P(T \mid \lnot V) = 0.1 \)
  • \( P(C \mid V) = 0.9 \), \( P(C \mid \lnot V) = 0.2 \)

The Burglary Alarm network

Five variables: Burglary (B), Earthquake (E), Alarm (A), JohnCalls (J), MaryCalls (M). Burglary and Earthquake both cause the Alarm. The Alarm (not the events themselves) causes John and Mary to call.

6. Computing a BN Query — Worked Example

The joint probability of any combination of values equals the product of the relevant CPT entries.

Worked example: P(W, ¬T, ¬V, C) step by step

We want the probability of: Good Weather AND No Toothache AND No Cavity AND Catch.

Use the factored form: \( P(W,\lnot T, \lnot V, C) = P(W) \cdot P(\lnot V) \cdot P(\lnot T \mid \lnot V) \cdot P(C \mid \lnot V) \)

Step 1 — P(W): Weather has no parents. From the CPT: \( P(W) = 0.75 \)

Step 2 — P(¬V): Cavity has no parents. \( P(\lnot V) = 1 - 0.2 = 0.8 \)

Step 3 — P(¬T | ¬V): Toothache given no cavity. \( P(\lnot T \mid \lnot V) = 1 - 0.1 = 0.9 \)

Step 4 — P(C | ¬V): Catch given no cavity. \( P(C \mid \lnot V) = 0.2 \)

Multiply: \( 0.75 \times 0.8 \times 0.9 \times 0.2 = \mathbf{0.108} \)

This works because the chain rule for the BN factorizes the joint. Each node only depends on its parents, so you only look up those CPT entries.

Exam tip — which factor goes where

Write the factored form before plugging in numbers. For each node, ask: "What are my parents?" Then look up the CPT entry for the exact combination of parent values specified in the query. Never forget to take the complement (\(1-p\)) for negated values.

7. Inference by Enumeration

Given a query variable \( Q \) and evidence \( E \), compute \( P(Q \mid E) \) by summing the joint over all unobserved (hidden) variables.

$$ P(Q \mid E) = \alpha \sum_{H} P(Q, E, H) $$

where \( \alpha = 1 / P(E) \) is the normalization constant and \( H \) ranges over all combinations of hidden variable values.

Burglary example — full trace

Query: \( P(B \mid J, M) \) — probability of burglary given both John and Mary called.

Step-by-step trace

Step 1: Hidden variables are A and E. Sum them out:

\( P(B,J,M) = \sum_A \sum_E P(J \mid A) \cdot P(M \mid A) \cdot P(A \mid B,E) \cdot P(B) \cdot P(E) \)

Step 2: Enumerate all 4 combinations of (A,E):

Term (A=T,E=T): \( 0.90 \times 0.70 \times 0.95 \times 0.001 \times 0.002 = 0.000001197 \)

Term (A=F,E=T): \( 0.05 \times 0.01 \times 0.05 \times 0.001 \times 0.002 = 5 \times 10^{-11} \)

Term (A=T,E=F): \( 0.90 \times 0.70 \times 0.94 \times 0.001 \times 0.998 \approx 0.000592 \)

Term (A=F,E=F): \( 0.05 \times 0.01 \times 0.06 \times 0.001 \times 0.998 \approx 0.0000003 \)

\( P(B,J,M) \approx 0.0005935 \)

Step 3: Repeat for \( P(\lnot B,J,M) \approx 0.001492 \)

Step 4: Normalize: \( P(B \mid J,M) = \dfrac{0.0005935}{0.0005935 + 0.001492} \approx \mathbf{0.2847} \)

Only a 28% chance of burglary despite both neighbors calling!

Complexity

Worst-case time and space: \( O(d^n) \) where \( d \) is the max domain size and \( n \) is the number of variables. For large networks this is prohibitive — it is the motivation for smarter methods.

8. Variable Elimination

Enumeration recomputes the same partial products repeatedly. Variable Elimination (VE) stores these as factors and reuses them.

Analogy — simplifying an algebraic expression

Instead of expanding \( (a+b)(c+d)(a+b) \) twice, you compute \( (a+b) \) once, store it, and use it twice. VE does the same thing for probability factors.

Key operations on factors

  • Pointwise product: \( f(X,Y) \times g(Y,Z) = h(X,Y,Z) \) — multiply entries that agree on shared variables.
  • Summing out: \( \sum_Y f(X,Y) = g(X) \) — marginalize out a hidden variable.

VE algorithm

FUNCTION VE(query Q, evidence E, network BN): factors ← [CPT for each variable, with evidence variables fixed] FOR each hidden variable H (in chosen elimination order): relevant_factors ← factors that mention H product ← pointwise_product(relevant_factors) new_factor ← sum_out(H, product) ← reuse! factors ← (factors − relevant_factors) ∪ {new_factor} result ← pointwise_product(remaining factors) RETURN normalize(result)

The elimination order matters enormously. A good order can reduce exponential to polynomial time. Choosing the optimal order is NP-hard, but good heuristics exist (min-degree, min-fill).

Exam tip — VE vs Enumeration

Both give exact answers. Enumeration is conceptually simpler. VE is faster for large networks because it caches intermediate factors. In the exam, if you are asked to compute a conditional probability in a BN, enumeration is usually expected unless VE is specifically asked.

9. Approximate Inference

For large BNs, even VE is intractable. Approximate methods use random sampling to estimate probabilities.

Rejection Sampling

Generate samples from the prior distribution. Reject any sample that contradicts the evidence. Estimate the posterior from the accepted samples.

Analogy — opinion poll with filter

Survey 10,000 people at random. Throw away the responses from people who don't match your target demographic. Estimate the opinion of the target group from what remains. Works — but wasteful if the target group is rare.

Weakness: If the evidence is rare (say, P(E) = 0.001), then 99.9% of samples are rejected. The estimate is slow to converge.

Likelihood Weighting

Fix the evidence variables to their observed values. Only sample the non-evidence variables. Weight each sample by the probability of the evidence given the non-evidence values.

$$ w(x) = \prod_{\text{evidence variable } E_i} P(E_i = e_i \mid \text{parents}(E_i)) $$
Why likelihood weighting is better

No samples are thrown away. Every sample contributes — just with different weight. The weighted average approximates the true conditional probability. Rejection sampling is a special case with all weights equal to 0 or 1.

Exam tip — which is better?

Likelihood weighting is almost always better than rejection sampling when the evidence is rare. Both converge to the correct answer as sample size grows. Neither is exact. Variable elimination is exact but may be slow.

10. Markov Chains

So far, every variable had a fixed value. Now we model a world that evolves over time. A Markov chain is a sequence of random variables where the future depends only on the present — not the whole past.

Markov assumption (first-order)

\( P(X_t \mid X_{t-1}, X_{t-2}, \ldots, X_0) = P(X_t \mid X_{t-1}) \)

The current state is all you need to predict the next state. History beyond one step is irrelevant.

The transition model specifies \( P(X_t \mid X_{t-1}) \) for all pairs. It can be written as a matrix called the Transition Probability Matrix (TPM).

Media / Computer example

A customer either buys Computer Electronics (C) or Media Accessories (M). The TPM is:

From \ ToComputer (C)Media (M)
Computer (C)0.400.60
Media (M)0.200.80

Three inference types from the slides

Type 1 — joint probability of a fixed sequence

P(C, M, M, C) starting from C: use the chain rule along the sequence.

\( P(C) \cdot P(M \mid C) \cdot P(M \mid M) \cdot P(C \mid M) = 1 \times 0.6 \times 0.8 \times 0.2 = \mathbf{0.096} \)

Type 2 — probability of a sequence starting from M

P(M, M, M, C): customer started at M, stays M twice, then switches to C.

\( P(M) \cdot P(M \mid M) \cdot P(M \mid M) \cdot P(C \mid M) = 1 \times 0.8 \times 0.8 \times 0.2 = \mathbf{0.128} \)

Type 3 — most likely future sequence (like Viterbi)

Starting from C, find the most likely 3-step future: maximize \( P(C, X, Y, Z) \).

Try all paths from C. The winner: C → M → M → M gives \( 1 \times 0.6 \times 0.8 \times 0.8 = \mathbf{0.384} \).

Answer: expected pattern = (C, M, M, M)

11. Hidden Markov Model

A Markov chain assumes you can see the state. A Hidden Markov Model (HMM) adds a twist: the state is hidden. You only see noisy observations.

The underground bunker story (from the slides)

A security guard is locked in an underground bunker with no windows. She cannot see the weather outside. But she can see whether colleagues bring umbrellas to work. The weather (Rainy / Sunny) is the hidden state. Umbrellas are the observation. She must infer the weather from umbrella patterns.

HMM components

An HMM has two kinds of variables at each time step \( t \):

  • \( X_t \) — the hidden state (e.g., weather: Low/High pressure)
  • \( E_t \) — the observation (e.g., barometer reading: Sunny/Rainy)

It is governed by three probability distributions:

  • Initial distribution: \( P(X_0) \)
  • Transition model: \( P(X_t \mid X_{t-1}) \) — how the hidden state evolves
  • Emission model: \( P(E_t \mid X_t) \) — how observations are generated from states

Example — pressure and weather

DistributionValues
Initial \( P(X_0) \)P(L)=0.5, P(H)=0.5
Transition \( P(X_t \mid X_{t-1}) \)P(L|L)=0.5, P(H|L)=0.5, P(L|H)=0.2, P(H|H)=0.8
Emission \( P(E_t \mid X_t) \)P(S|L)=0.2, P(R|L)=0.8, P(S|H)=0.6, P(R|H)=0.4
The four HMM inference tasks
  • Filtering: \( P(X_t \mid E_{1:t}) \) — what is the current state given all evidence so far?
  • Prediction: \( P(X_{t+k} \mid E_{1:t}) \) — what will the future state be?
  • Smoothing: \( P(X_k \mid E_{1:t}) \) for \( k < t \) — what was the past state, given all evidence including future?
  • Most likely explanation: \( \text{argmax}_{X_{1:t}} P(X_{1:t} \mid E_{1:t}) \) — what is the single best state sequence?

12. Filtering — the Forward Algorithm

Filtering computes \( P(X_t \mid E_{1:t}) \) — your belief about the current state given all past and present observations. The forward algorithm does this recursively, one time step at a time.

$$ \alpha_t(x) = P(E_t \mid x) \sum_{x'} P(x \mid x') \cdot \alpha_{t-1}(x') $$

where \( \alpha_t(x) \) is the (unnormalized) forward variable for state \( x \) at time \( t \). After each step, normalize so probabilities sum to 1.

Worked example — sequence S-S-R

Hidden states: L (Low pressure), H (High pressure). Observations: S (Sunny), R (Rainy). Compute \( P(\text{sequence S-S-R}) \).

Step-by-step forward pass

Initialization (t=1, obs=S):

\( \alpha_1(L) = P(L) \cdot P(S \mid L) = 0.5 \times 0.2 = 0.1 \quad \rightarrow \text{normalized: } 0.25 \)

\( \alpha_1(H) = P(H) \cdot P(S \mid H) = 0.5 \times 0.6 = 0.3 \quad \rightarrow \text{normalized: } 0.75 \)

Step 2 (t=2, obs=S):

\( \alpha_2(L) = [\alpha_1(L) \cdot P(L \mid L) + \alpha_1(H) \cdot P(L \mid H)] \cdot P(S \mid L) \)

\( = [0.25 \times 0.5 + 0.75 \times 0.2] \times 0.2 = [0.125 + 0.15] \times 0.2 = 0.055 \)

\( \alpha_2(H) = [0.25 \times 0.5 + 0.75 \times 0.8] \times 0.6 = [0.125 + 0.6] \times 0.6 = 0.435 \)

Step 3 (t=3, obs=R):

\( \alpha_3(L) = [\alpha_2(L) \cdot P(L \mid L) + \alpha_2(H) \cdot P(L \mid H)] \cdot P(R \mid L) \)

\( = [0.055 \times 0.5 + 0.435 \times 0.2] \times 0.8 = [0.0275 + 0.087] \times 0.8 = 0.0916 \)

\( \alpha_3(H) = [0.055 \times 0.5 + 0.435 \times 0.8] \times 0.4 = [0.0275 + 0.348] \times 0.4 = 0.1502 \)

Termination: \( P(\text{S-S-R}) = \alpha_3(L) + \alpha_3(H) = 0.0916 + 0.1502 = \mathbf{0.2418} \)

Exam tip — normalization

The forward algorithm normalizes at each step. After step 1, 0.1 and 0.3 become 0.25 and 0.75. These normalized values are used in step 2. If you use the raw (unnormalized) values throughout, you get the joint probability \( P(E_{1:t}, X_t) \), not the conditional \( P(X_t \mid E_{1:t}) \). The final total \( P(\text{sequence}) \) is computed from the unnormalized values.

13. Viterbi Algorithm — Most Likely State Sequence

Filtering asks "what is the most likely current state?" Viterbi asks "what is the single most likely sequence of states that explains the observations?" This is Inference Type 3 from the slides, generalized to HMMs.

Analogy — the best route through a city

You want the single fastest route from A to Z through several intersections. You could enumerate all paths — exponential. Or you could use dynamic programming: at each intersection, keep only the best partial path arriving there. Viterbi does exactly this through the state space.

Algorithm

$$ \delta_t(x) = \max_{x'} \left[ \delta_{t-1}(x') \cdot P(x \mid x') \right] \cdot P(E_t \mid x) $$

where \( \delta_t(x) \) is the probability of the most likely path that ends in state \( x \) at time \( t \). At each step, instead of summing (as in forward algorithm), you take the maximum.

Forward vs Viterbi — one symbol difference
  • Forward: \( \alpha_t(x) = P(E_t \mid x) \cdot \mathbf{\sum}_{x'} P(x \mid x') \cdot \alpha_{t-1}(x') \)
  • Viterbi: \( \delta_t(x) = P(E_t \mid x) \cdot \mathbf{\max}_{x'} P(x \mid x') \cdot \delta_{t-1}(x') \)

Sum → total probability of all paths. Max → probability of the best path. This single change switches from filtering to most-likely-explanation.

Worked example — Markov chain Type 3 as Viterbi

Starting from C, observe 3 more purchases. Find the most likely sequence.

\( \delta_1(C) = 1, \quad \delta_1(M) = 0 \)

\( \delta_2(C) = \max[\delta_1(C) \cdot P(C \mid C), \delta_1(M) \cdot P(C \mid M)] = \max[1 \times 0.4, 0] = 0.4 \)

\( \delta_2(M) = \max[\delta_1(C) \cdot P(M \mid C), \delta_1(M) \cdot P(M \mid M)] = \max[1 \times 0.6, 0] = 0.6 \)

\( \delta_3(M) = \max[0.4 \times 0.6, 0.6 \times 0.8] = \max[0.24, 0.48] = 0.48 \) (from M)

\( \delta_4(M) = \max[0.4 \times 0.6 \times 0.8, 0.6 \times 0.8 \times 0.8] = \max[0.192, 0.384] = 0.384 \) (from M)

Best path traceback: C → M → M → M with probability 0.384. ✓ Matches slides.

Backpointer — don't forget this

During the forward sweep, store the \( \text{argmax} \) state at each step. After reaching the end, follow the backpointers to reconstruct the actual best path. Without backpointers you have the probability but not the path.

ML connections

  • Naive Bayes classifier: a BN where all features are conditionally independent given the class. Fast, surprisingly effective.
  • HMM in NLP: used for POS tagging (hidden state = grammar tag, observation = word), speech recognition (hidden = phoneme, observation = audio frame), and gene sequencing.
  • Kalman filter: a Gaussian continuous-state HMM used in robotics and tracking.
  • Deep Learning + HMM: modern ASR systems use neural networks to estimate emission probabilities, combined with the HMM structure for sequence decoding.
Final exam reminders
  • For BN queries, always write the factored form first, then substitute values.
  • Forward algorithm uses \( \sum \); Viterbi uses \( \max \). One symbol difference.
  • Conditional independence is the key structural property exploited by BNs.
  • Inference Types 1/2/3 from slides map to: joint probability, joint from different start, Viterbi-style best path.
  • P(B|JM) ≈ 0.2847 — only ~28%, because burglaries are rare (P(B)=0.001).
