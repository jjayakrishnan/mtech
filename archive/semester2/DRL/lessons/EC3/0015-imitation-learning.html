Why Imitation Learning?

Reinforcement learning assumes you can specify a reward function: "do this, avoid that." But for many real-world tasks, the reward is extraordinarily hard to write down — yet a human expert can perform the task effortlessly.

Everyday picture — teaching by showing

Imagine teaching someone to ride a bike by writing down a formula for balance. It's nearly impossible. But watching an expert ride — and then imitating — works in minutes. Imitation Learning is the formal version of this idea: learn a policy directly from expert demonstrations, without ever defining a reward function.

Hard-to-specify reward examples

  • Surgical robotics: "make the incision correctly" is not a scalar. A surgeon can demonstrate the right trajectory; specifying tissue-stress, angle, depth, and force numerically is intractable.
  • Autonomous driving: "drive well" involves thousands of trade-offs — comfort, safety, legality, social norms. Expert drivers navigate all of these implicitly; any single reward misses something.
  • Robotic manipulation: "pick up the object gracefully" — what does graceful mean, precisely, in reward form?
Formal goal of imitation learning

Given a dataset of expert demonstrations \(\mathcal{D} = \{(s_1, a_1^*), (s_2, a_2^*), \ldots\}\), learn a policy \(\pi_\theta\) that behaves like the expert — i.e. produces similar actions in similar states — without access to the reward function \(R(s, a)\).

There are three main approaches, each making different assumptions and trade-offs:

  1. Behavioural Cloning (BC): pure supervised learning on demo data.
  2. DAgger: interactive data collection; asks the expert during training.
  3. Inverse RL / GAIL: recover the reward or its surrogate from demos, then run RL.

Behavioural Cloning

Behavioural Cloning (BC) is the simplest possible approach: treat imitation as supervised learning. Each expert demonstration gives us a state–action pair \((s, a^*)\). Train a policy network to predict \(a^*\) given \(s\).

The learning objective

\[ \pi_\theta^{\text{BC}} = \arg\min_\theta \;\sum_{(s,\,a^*)\,\in\,\mathcal{D}} \mathcal{L}\!\left(\pi_\theta(s),\; a^*\right) \]

where \(\mathcal{L}\) is cross-entropy loss for discrete actions or MSE for continuous actions. Nothing new here — it is exactly image classification or regression, just with states as inputs and actions as labels.

BC algorithm
  1. Collect demonstrations \(\mathcal{D} = \{(s_i, a_i^*)\}_{i=1}^N\) from a human or scripted expert.
  2. Train \(\pi_\theta\) by minimising \(\mathcal{L}(\pi_\theta(s_i), a_i^*)\) via SGD.
  3. Deploy \(\pi_\theta\) directly — no RL loop needed.

Why it can work

BC is fast, simple, and requires no environment interaction during training. With enough high-quality demonstrations it can produce competent behaviour — as shown in impressive results on Atari and dexterous manipulation with large datasets.

Why it often fails — compounding errors

The fatal flaw is compounding errors. BC only sees the states the expert visits. The expert never makes mistakes, so the training distribution is \(p_{\text{expert}}(s)\). When deployed, any small mistake shifts the agent to a state the expert never visited. The policy has no idea what to do — it makes another mistake — which shifts to an even more unfamiliar state — and so on.

\[ \text{Error at step } T \;\propto\; \epsilon \cdot T^2 \]

where \(\epsilon\) is the per-step error rate. Errors compound quadratically with episode length. A policy 99% accurate per step can still be catastrophically wrong after 100 steps.

Exam tip

BC's key failure is covariate shift (also called distribution shift). The training distribution \(p_{\text{expert}}(s)\) does not match the deployment distribution \(p_\pi(s)\). BC has no mechanism to recover from errors because it never learned from error states.

Distribution Shift and Covariate Shift

Distribution shift is the core problem with BC. It deserves its own section because understanding it precisely is what motivates every more sophisticated method.

Covariate shift — the precise statement

During training, BC sees states sampled from the expert's state distribution \(p_{\text{expert}}(s)\). At deployment, the agent's own actions generate states from \(p_\pi(s)\). These two distributions are different — even a small policy error creates this gap, and the gap widens with each step.

The drift mechanism

Think of a 1-D track. The expert drives perfectly along the centre. BC trains on those centre-line states. At test time, a tiny perturbation (wind, sensor noise) shifts the car slightly off-centre. Now the car is in a state the expert never visited. BC's policy, trained only on expert states, outputs a poorly calibrated action. The car drifts further. Repeat — and the car is eventually in the weeds.

Why this is hard to fix with more data

You might think: "collect more expert data and the problem goes away." It doesn't. The expert only ever demonstrates the right trajectory — they never show the agent how to recover from mistakes. No matter how many expert demonstrations you collect, \(p_{\text{expert}}(s)\) never includes error-recovery states.

Key insight

The fix is not more expert demonstrations of the same thing. It is demonstrations of what to do when the agent is off the expert's path. That is exactly what DAgger provides.

DAgger — Dataset Aggregation

DAgger (Dataset Aggregation, Ross et al. 2011) directly addresses distribution shift by iteratively collecting data under the agent's own distribution and asking the expert for corrections.

The core idea

Instead of training once on a fixed expert dataset, DAgger runs in rounds. In each round: let the current policy explore, observe which states it visits, ask the expert "what should the action be here?", add those labelled states to the dataset, retrain. After enough rounds, the policy's state distribution converges toward the expert's distribution — the shift vanishes.

DAgger algorithm
  1. Collect initial dataset \(\mathcal{D}_0\) with expert demonstrations. Train \(\pi_0\).
  2. For each iteration \(i = 1, 2, \ldots, N\):
    1. Run \(\pi_{i-1}\) in the environment to collect trajectory states \(\{s_1, \ldots, s_T\}\).
    2. Query the expert: obtain \(a_t^* = \pi^*(s_t)\) for each visited state.
    3. Aggregate: \(\mathcal{D}_i = \mathcal{D}_{i-1} \cup \{(s_t, a_t^*)\}\).
    4. Train new policy: \(\pi_i = \arg\min_\pi \mathcal{L}(\pi, \mathcal{D}_i)\).
  3. Return the best policy from all iterations (or average over a mix).
Input: Expert π*, environment, N iterations
Initialise: D ← expert demonstrations; train π₀ on D

For i = 1 to N:
  τ ← rollout(πᵢ₋₁) // run current policy
  labels ← {(s, π*(s)) : s ∈ τ} // query expert at visited states
  D ← D ∪ labels // aggregate
  πᵢ ← argmin_π L(π, D) // retrain on full dataset

Return: best πᵢ

Convergence guarantee

Ross et al. prove that DAgger converges to a policy with error \(O(\epsilon)\) rather than BC's \(O(\epsilon T^2)\), where \(\epsilon\) is the per-step imitiation loss. The quadratic blow-up vanishes because the dataset now covers the states the agent actually visits.

Analogy — supervised driving lessons

BC is like reading a driving manual. DAgger is like having an instructor beside you during your actual drives — every time you drift toward the kerb, they say "now turn left." After enough lessons, you've received expert guidance at every kind of situation you actually encounter.

Trade-off: expert queries required

DAgger's cost is online expert access. You need a mechanism to ask the expert "what would you do here?" for arbitrary states. In robotics this means a human operator in the loop during training. This is expensive. BC needs the expert only once (upfront). DAgger needs the expert at every iteration.

Exam tip

DAgger reduces error from \(O(\epsilon T^2)\) to \(O(\epsilon T)\) — the key complexity result. The price paid is online expert queries. If the exam asks "when would you prefer BC over DAgger?" — the answer is when an interactive expert is unavailable (pre-recorded demos only).

Inverse Reinforcement Learning

Both BC and DAgger learn a policy directly from demonstrations. Inverse RL (IRL) takes a different path: recover the reward function that the expert is implicitly optimising, then use RL to find a policy that maximises it.

The IRL problem

Given: MDP without reward, expert trajectories \(\{\tau_1, \tau_2, \ldots\}\).
Find: reward function \(R^*(s, a)\) such that the expert is (near-)optimal under \(R^*\).
Then: solve the forward RL problem with \(R^*\) to get a policy \(\pi\).

Why recover the reward, not just the policy?

A learned reward function generalises. If the environment changes slightly (new layout, different start state), the policy from BC/DAgger must be retrained from scratch. A reward function can be transferred to the new environment and re-optimised. This is the principled advantage of IRL over direct imitation.

Maximum Entropy IRL

The most widely used IRL variant is Maximum Entropy IRL (Ziebart et al. 2008). It resolves the ambiguity in IRL (many reward functions can explain the same demos) by choosing the reward that matches expert feature expectations while being maximally uncertain about everything else.

\[ R^* = \arg\max_R \;\mathbb{E}_{\pi^*}\!\left[\sum_t R(s_t, a_t)\right] - \log Z(R) \]

where \(Z(R)\) is the partition function (normalising constant) over all possible trajectories. Maximising this objective forces the reward to match the expert's trajectory returns exactly, without over-fitting to a single explanation.

Feature expectation matching — the key constraint

Represent state–action pairs as feature vectors \(\phi(s, a)\). The reward is assumed linear: \(R(s, a) = \theta^\top \phi(s, a)\). IRL finds \(\theta\) such that:

\[ \mathbb{E}_{\pi^*}\!\left[\sum_t \phi(s_t, a_t)\right] = \mathbb{E}_{\pi_\theta}\!\left[\sum_t \phi(s_t, a_t)\right] \]

Expert and agent have the same feature counts — the agent cannot be distinguished from the expert on the basis of \(\phi\).

Computational cost

MaxEnt IRL requires solving a full forward RL problem in the inner loop at every gradient step. This is prohibitively expensive for large state spaces. Each gradient update on \(\theta\) requires re-running policy optimisation to convergence. That is the main practical drawback of IRL compared to BC/DAgger.

Exam tip

IRL is "more principled" because it recovers the reward — but this comes at the cost of solving RL in an inner loop. The exam may ask you to compare IRL's sample complexity and computational cost against BC/DAgger. IRL requires both expert demos and environment interaction during training.

GAIL — Generative Adversarial Imitation Learning

GAIL (Ho & Ermon, 2016) sidesteps the expensive inner RL loop of IRL by borrowing a structure from GANs. Instead of recovering a reward function and then running RL, GAIL trains a policy and a discriminator simultaneously in an adversarial loop.

The discriminator

A discriminator \(D_\psi(s, a)\) is a binary classifier: given a state–action pair, output the probability that it came from the expert (vs the agent). The discriminator is trained to separate expert transitions from agent transitions.

\[ \max_D\; \mathbb{E}_{\pi^*}\!\left[\log D(s, a)\right] + \mathbb{E}_{\pi_\theta}\!\left[\log\!\left(1 - D(s, a)\right)\right] \]

The policy

The policy \(\pi_\theta\) is trained (via PPO or TRPO) to fool the discriminator — to make its transitions look like expert transitions. The policy's reward signal is \(\log D(s, a)\): the higher the discriminator's expert-probability for a transition, the better.

\[ \min_\pi\; \max_D\; \mathbb{E}_{\pi^*}\!\left[\log D(s, a)\right] + \mathbb{E}_{\pi}\!\left[\log\!\left(1 - D(s, a)\right)\right] \]
Connection to GANs

In image GANs: generator produces fake images; discriminator separates real from fake; generator trains to fool discriminator.
In GAIL: policy produces agent transitions; discriminator separates expert from agent; policy trains to fool discriminator.
The adversarial game has the same fixed point: \(D(s,a) = 0.5\) everywhere — the agent is indistinguishable from the expert.

Connection to IRL

Ho & Ermon show that GAIL is equivalent to IRL with an occupancy measure matching objective, where the GAN loss plays the role of a reward-recovery step. GAIL avoids the explicit inner-loop reward fitting — the discriminator implicitly encodes the reward signal without ever committing to a particular \(R(s,a)\) form.

Trade-offs

GAIL requires environment interaction (like IRL), but avoids the inner RL loop (unlike IRL). It scales better to high-dimensional continuous action spaces and has produced state-of-the-art results on MuJoCo locomotion tasks. The downside: adversarial training instability — the discriminator and policy can fail to converge if training is not carefully tuned.

Exam tip

The GAIL fixed point is \(D(s,a) = 0.5\) everywhere — this is the convergence criterion, meaning agent and expert are indistinguishable. If the exam asks "when has GAIL converged?" the answer is when the discriminator cannot do better than random guessing.

BC vs DAgger vs IRL vs GAIL

Here is a side-by-side summary of the four approaches. This is the kind of comparison table that appears in exam questions.

Property Behavioural Cloning DAgger Inverse RL GAIL
Expert queries during training? No — upfront demos only Yes — at every iteration No — upfront demos only No — upfront demos only
Reward function needed? No No No (recovers it) No (bypasses it)
Environment interaction during training? No Yes Yes (inner RL loop) Yes
Scalability (large state spaces) Good — just supervised learning Moderate — needs online rollouts Poor — inner RL loop is expensive Good — GAN avoids inner loop
Convergence guarantee None — quadratic error growth \(O(\epsilon T^2)\) Yes — error \(O(\epsilon T)\) after N iterations Yes (under feature matching) Conditional — if adversarial training converges
Transfers to new environment? No — policy is fixed No — policy is fixed Yes — reward generalises No — policy is fixed
Training stability Stable (standard SGD) Stable (standard SGD) Moderate (alternating optimisation) Can be unstable (adversarial)
Best used when… Large dataset of demos, no env access Interactive expert available, shorter episodes Need transferable reward, small state space High-dim continuous control, env access
Practical advice — the quick-decision rule
  • No environment access at all → Behavioural Cloning
  • Can interact with the environment and query an expert → DAgger
  • Need the reward to transfer or interpret → Inverse RL
  • High-dimensional continuous control, need scalability → GAIL
One-line memory hooks
  • BC: "copy the expert's moves" — simple, brittle at deployment
  • DAgger: "ask the expert to correct my mistakes" — fixes distribution shift
  • IRL: "figure out what the expert is trying to achieve" — most principled
  • GAIL: "pretend to be the expert until no one can tell the difference" — adversarial, scalable
