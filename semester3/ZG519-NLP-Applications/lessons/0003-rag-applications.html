The big idea Book §11.4

Analogy — open-book exam A plain LLM sits a closed-book exam: it answers from memory and sometimes bluffs. RAG turns it into an open-book exam: first a librarian (the retriever) fetches the right pages, then the student (the generator, an LLM) writes the answer using those pages. The book uses exactly these terms: open book QA (with retrieved documents) vs closed book QA (from the model alone).
User question ─► [Retriever] ─► top-k passages ─┐ ├─► [Prompt] ─► [LLM generator] ─► answer (+ citations) User question ────────────────────────────────┘ ▲ document collection (indexed in advance)

Why bother? The book lists three goals:

  1. Reduce hallucination by giving the model a set of trusted documents.
  2. Answer about private data — personal email, health records, company documents, legal files — that the model never saw in training.
  3. Handle time-sensitive knowledge — facts newer than the model's training data.

Information retrieval basics Book §11.1

The whole idea: given a query, return a ranked list of documents from a collection, most relevant first. The task is called ad hoc retrieval.

TermMeaning
DocumentWhatever unit is indexed — a web page, paper, or just a paragraph/passage
CollectionThe set of documents searched (the whole web, a company repo, one person's files)
TermA word (or phrase) in the collection
QueryThe user's information need written as a set of terms

Two families, depending on how documents and queries become vectors:

Sparse retrievalDense retrieval
VectorsWord-count vectors weighted by tf-idf or BM25 (mostly zeros, length = vocabulary)Embeddings from a language model (a few hundred numbers, all non-zero)
MatchesExact word overlap onlyMeaning — handles synonyms
Speed trickInverted indexApproximate nearest-neighbour search (Faiss)

Bag of words, term–document matrix

The vector space model (Salton, 1971) represents a document as a vector of word counts and ignores word order — a "bag of words". Stack many documents and you get a term–document matrix: rows are words, columns are documents.

Each column is a document vector. The two comedies, As You Like It [1, 114, 36, 20] and Twelfth Night [0, 80, 58, 15], look alike (many fools and wits, few battles) — far more than either looks like Julius Caesar [7, 62, 1, 2] or Henry V [13, 89, 4, 3]. Similar words ⇒ similar vectors.

tf-idf weighting and cosine score Book §11.1.2–11.1.3

Raw counts mislead: the word good is frequent everywhere. We want weights that say how informative a word is for a document.

$$ \text{tf}_{t,d}=\begin{cases}1+\log_{10}\text{count}(t,d) & \text{count}(t,d)>0\\ 0 & \text{otherwise}\end{cases}\qquad \text{idf}_t=\log_{10}\frac{N}{\text{df}_t}\qquad \text{tf-idf}(t,d)=\text{tf}_{t,d}\cdot\text{idf}_t $$
  • tf (term frequency): log-squashed count — 1 time → 1, 10 times → 2, 100 times → 3, 1000 times → 4. A word appearing 100× is not 100× as important.
  • df (document frequency): number of documents containing the term; idf is high for rare terms, and exactly 0 for a term in every document (good, sweet in Shakespeare, \( N=37 \)). The book's idf values: Romeo 1.57 (in 1 play), salad 1.27, Falstaff 0.967, forest 0.489, battle 0.246, wit 0.037, fool 0.012, good 0.

Score a document by the cosine between query and document vectors (normalised dot product), so long documents don't win just by being long:

$$ \text{score}(q,d)=\cos(q,d)=\frac{q\cdot d}{|q|\,|d|}=\sum_{t\in q}\frac{\text{tf-idf}(t,q)}{\sqrt{\sum_{q_i}\text{tf-idf}^2(q_i,q)}}\cdot\frac{\text{tf-idf}(t,d)}{\sqrt{\sum_{d_i}\text{tf-idf}^2(d_i,d)}} $$

Worked exampleThe book's four nano-documents (Fig. 11.6)

Query: sweet love. Documents: D1 sweet sweet nurse love · D2 sweet sorrow · D3 how sweet is love · D4 nurse. \( N=4 \).

Step 1 — idf. df: sweet 3, love 2, nurse 2, how/sorrow/is 1. So idf: sweet \( \log_{10}(4/3)=0.125 \); love \( \log_{10}(4/2)=0.301 \); nurse 0.301; how, sorrow, is \( \log_{10}4=0.602 \).

Step 2 — query vector. Each query word occurs once → tf = 1. tf-idf: sweet 0.125, love 0.301. Length \( |q|=\sqrt{0.125^2+0.301^2}=0.326 \). Normalised: sweet 0.383, love 0.924.

Step 3 — D1. sweet appears twice → tf \( =1+\log_{10}2=1.301 \), tf-idf \( =1.301\times0.125=0.163 \). nurse: tf 1 → 0.301. love: 0.301. Length \( |d_1|=\sqrt{0.163^2+0.301^2+0.301^2}=0.456 \). Normalised: sweet 0.357, nurse 0.661, love 0.661.

Step 4 — dot product with the normalised query (only terms in both): \( 0.383\times0.357+0.924\times0.661=0.137+0.610=\mathbf{0.747} \).

Step 5 — D2. tf-idf: sweet 0.125, sorrow 0.602; \( |d_2|=0.615 \); normalised sweet 0.203. Score \( =0.383\times0.203=\mathbf{0.0779} \).

The exercise the book leaves to you: D3 = 0.358, D4 = 0 (no shared word). Ranking: D1 (0.747) > D3 (0.358) > D2 (0.078) > D4 (0).

Variants (book eq. 11.11): drop the second idf copy on the document side (it is already in the query) — sometimes works better. Stop words (the, a, to) were once removed; modern systems mostly keep them because idf already down-weights them — but "to be or not to be" becomes just "not" if you remove them.

BM25 Book eq. 11.12

BM25 (Okapi) is the improved tf-idf: it stops term frequency from growing without limit and compensates for document length.

$$ \text{score}(q,d)=\sum_{t\in q}\ \underbrace{\log\frac{N}{\text{df}_t}}_{\text{idf}}\ \cdot\ \underbrace{\frac{\text{tf}_{t,d}}{k\left(1-b+b\frac{|d|}{|d_{\text{avg}}|}\right)+\text{tf}_{t,d}}}_{\text{saturating, length-normalised tf}} $$
  • \( k \) balances tf vs idf. \( k=0 \): ignore term frequency (only whether the term occurs). Large \( k \): approaches raw tf. Typical \( k\in[1.2,2] \).
  • \( b \) controls length normalisation: 1 = fully scale by length, 0 = none. Typical \( b=0.75 \).
  • In this formula \( \text{tf}_{t,d} \) is the raw count in the document.

Worked exampleBM25 on the same four documents (k = 1.2, b = 0.75)

Document lengths: D1 = 4, D2 = 2, D3 = 4, D4 = 1 words → \( |d_{\text{avg}}|=11/4=2.75 \). idf: sweet 0.125, love 0.301.

D1 (|d| = 4): length factor \( k(1-b+b\cdot 4/2.75)=1.2(0.25+1.0909)=1.609 \).
sweet (count 2): \( 0.125\times 2/(1.609+2)=0.0693 \). love (count 1): \( 0.301\times1/(1.609+1)=0.1153 \). Score 0.185.

D3 (|d| = 4): same length factor 1.609. sweet: \( 0.125\times1/2.609=0.0479 \); love: 0.1153 → 0.163.

D2 (|d| = 2): factor \( 1.2(0.25+0.75\times2/2.75)=0.9545 \). sweet: \( 0.125/1.9545=0.0640 \) → 0.064. D4 → 0.

Same order as tf-idf (D1 > D3 > D2 > D4). Notice D1's double "sweet" helps only a little — saturation.

Try it: the Marriage of Figaro passage can outrank the correct one. Two reasons, both classic sparse-retrieval weaknesses: (1) the query word premiere does not match premiered — there is no stemming, so an important word scores zero; (2) in this tiny collection the word of is rare, so it gets a high idf even though it carries no meaning (stop-word problem). The inverted index Book §11.1.4

Scoring every document for every query is wasteful: any document with none of the query words scores 0 and can be skipped. The inverted index flips the table: a dictionary of terms, each pointing to a postings list of the documents containing it (with counts, and optionally positions). Like the index at the back of a book.

how {1} → 3 [1] is {1} → 3 [1] love {2} → 1 [1] → 3 [1] nurse {2} → 1 [1] → 4 [1] sorrow {1} → 2 [1] sweet {3} → 1 [2] → 2 [1] → 3 [1] ← term {df} → doc [count] → doc [count] …

For the query sweet love: take the postings of sweet (docs 1, 2, 3) and love (docs 1, 3); candidates = {1, 2, 3}. D4 is never even looked at. The index already stores df and counts, so idf and tf are free.

Ranked retrieval in one paragraph: build the inverted index once; for a query, find candidate documents from the postings, score each with tf-idf/BM25 cosine, sort high→low, return the top N.

Evaluating retrieval Book §11.2

With \( T \) returned documents, \( R \) of them relevant, and \( U \) relevant documents in the whole collection:

$$ \text{Precision}=\frac{|R|}{|T|}\qquad\text{Recall}=\frac{|R|}{|U|} $$

These ignore ranking, so we compute them at each rank \( k \): precision\(_k\) = (relevant in top \( k \)) / \( k \); recall\(_k\) = (relevant in top \( k \)) / \( U \). Recall never decreases as you go down; precision zig-zags.

Judgments (R = relevant, N = not) in rank order: R N R N R R N R N N R N N N R N N R N N N N N N R. Relevant ranks: 1, 3, 5, 6, 8, 11, 15, 18, 25.

Rank123568101125
Precision1.00.500.670.600.670.630.500.550.36
Recall.11.11.22.33.44.56.56.671.0

E.g. rank 5: 3 relevant (ranks 1, 3, 5) → precision \( 3/5=0.60 \), recall \( 3/9=0.33 \).

Interpolated precision at recall \( r \) = the maximum precision at any recall \( \ge r \): \( \text{IntPrecision}(r)=\max_{i\ge r}\text{Precision}(i) \). It smooths the zig-zag and lets you average over queries (11 recall levels 0, .1, …, 1.0).

Average precision (AP) = the mean of the precision values measured only at ranks where a relevant document appears (ranks 1, 3, 5, 6, 8, 11, 15, 18, 25):

\( \text{AP}=\dfrac{1+0.667+0.6+0.667+0.625+0.545+0.467+0.444+0.36}{9}=\dfrac{5.375}{9}\approx\mathbf{0.60} \) ✓ (book: 0.6). MAP = the mean of AP over all queries.

Dense retrieval Book §11.3

The vocabulary-mismatch problem You search "cheap car" but the page says "affordable automobile". tf-idf finds no shared word and scores it 0. Dense retrieval compares meaning (embeddings), so the two land close together.

Worked exampleWhy embeddings fix it (designed toy example)

Suppose 2-D unit embeddings: query "car" \( q=(0.8, 0.6) \); doc A "automobile engine" \( d_A=(0.6,0.8) \); doc B "banana bread" \( d_B=(-0.6,0.8) \).

Sparse overlap with "car": A = 0, B = 0 (no shared words) — can't tell them apart.

Dense dot product: \( q\cdot d_A=0.48+0.48=\mathbf{0.96} \); \( q\cdot d_B=-0.48+0.48=\mathbf{0} \). Document A wins.

Retrieval is done on passages (e.g. non-overlapping chunks of ~100 tokens), not whole documents. Three architectures, in order of cost:

ArchitectureHow it worksAccuracyCost
Cross-encoder (full BERT)Feed query [SEP] document to one BERT; a linear layer on the [CLS] output gives the score. Self-attention sees every query–document word pair. Query ≤ 64 tokens, whole input ≤ 512BestEvery query must be paired with every document — impractical except for re-ranking a short list
Bi-encoderTwo encoders: \( z_q=\text{BERT}_Q(q)[\text{CLS}] \), \( z_d=\text{BERT}_D(d)[\text{CLS}] \); score = dot product \( z_q\cdot z_d \). Documents are encoded once, in advanceLower (no token-level interaction)Cheapest — encode only the query at search time
ColBERTSeparate encoders, but keep a vector per token (unit length). Score = sum over query tokens of the best-matching document token (MaxSim)Between the twoDocument token vectors pre-stored
$$ \text{score}_{\text{ColBERT}}(q,d)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\max_{j=1}^{m}\ E_{q_i}\cdot E_{d_j} $$

Worked exampleMaxSim with tiny vectors (designed)

Query tokens: \( q_1=(1,0) \), \( q_2=(0,1) \). Document tokens: \( d_1=(0.8,0.6) \), \( d_2=(0.6,0.8) \), \( d_3=(-1,0) \).

For \( q_1 \): dot products with \( d_1,d_2,d_3 \) = 0.8, 0.6, −1 → max = 0.8. For \( q_2 \): 0.6, 0.8, 0 → max = 0.8.

Score \( =0.8+0.8=\mathbf{1.6} \). A relevant document is one where each query word finds a similar word.

  • Pipeline compromise: use cheap BM25 to get the top-N, then rerank only those N with the expensive cross-encoder.
  • Training data: supervised retrievers need (query, relevant passage, irrelevant passage) examples — e.g. MS MARCO. Negatives can be sampled from an existing system's top-1000. ColBERT trains on triples \( \langle q,d^+,d^-\rangle \) with cross-entropy loss.
  • Efficiency: finding the document vectors with the highest dot product is nearest-neighbour search; real systems use approximate nearest neighbour such as Faiss. Brute force for 1 million passages × 768 dimensions is 768 million multiply-adds per query.

The RAG algorithm Book §11.4

Two components: the retriever and the generator (historically the "reader"). The basic algorithm for a document collection \( D \) and query \( q \):

  1. Retrieve \( R(q)=d_1\dots d_k \): the top-\( k \) passages from \( D \).
  2. Build a prompt containing the passages and \( q \).
  3. Call the LLM, which generates the answer conditioned on the prompt:
$$ p(x_1,\dots,x_n)=\prod_{i=1}^{n} p\big(x_i\mid R(q);\ \text{"Answer the following question…"};\ q;\ x_{<i}\big) $$
retrieved passage 1 retrieved passage 2 ... retrieved passage k Based on these texts, answer this question: What year was the premiere of The Magic Flute?

The basic version needs no training: take an off-the-shelf LLM and hope it uses the right passages.

Worked exampleHow many chunks? (designed, typical exam style)

A 1,000-token document is split into chunks of 200 tokens with 50 tokens of overlap (so a sentence cut at a boundary appears whole in the next chunk).

Step 1. Stride (how far the window moves) \( =200-50=150 \).

Step 2. Starts at 0, 150, 300, 450, 600, 750 cover up to token 950; a 7th chunk starting at 900 reaches the end.

Step 3. Formula: \( \lceil (1000-200)/150\rceil+1=\lceil5.33\rceil+1=\mathbf{7} \) chunks.

Beyond the book: Naive, Advanced, Graph and Agentic RAG Beyond the book

CautionGeneral knowledge, not from T1

The handout names Naïve RAG, Graph RAG, Agentic RAG and real-world case studies for this session, sourced from papers and the web. The textbook covers only the basic pipeline and a one-paragraph mention of agent-based RAG. What follows is the standard picture in the literature — match it against the professor's slides.

Naïve RAG — the pipeline you build first

  1. Index (offline): load documents → clean → chunk → embed each chunk → store vectors (+ text and metadata) in a vector database.
  2. Retrieve (online): embed the question, fetch the top-\( k \) nearest chunks.
  3. Generate: stuff the chunks into the prompt; the LLM answers (ideally with citations).

Where it fails: bad chunk boundaries cut an answer in half; the right chunk isn't in the top-\( k \); retrieved text is irrelevant yet the LLM trusts it; questions that need many documents ("what are the main themes across all of these reports?"); stale index; no check that the answer is grounded.

Advanced RAG — fixes around the same loop

  • Query rewriting / expansion (clean up spelling and ambiguity — connecting back to Session 2).
  • Hybrid search: run sparse (BM25) and dense retrieval, then merge. A common merge is Reciprocal Rank Fusion.
  • Reranking the merged list with a cross-encoder (the book's re-ranking idea).
  • Better chunking (by section/sentence), metadata filters, compressing long passages.

Worked exampleReciprocal Rank Fusion (designed)

RRF gives each document \( \sum_{\text{lists}}\dfrac{1}{c+\text{rank}} \) with a constant \( c=60 \). BM25 ranks: d1, d2, d3, d4. Dense ranks: d3, d1, d5, d2.

DocBM25 rankDense rankRRF score
d1121/61 + 1/62 = 0.03252
d3311/63 + 1/61 = 0.03227
d2241/62 + 1/64 = 0.03175
d5—31/63 = 0.01587

Fused order: d1, d3, d2, d5, d4. A document that is good in both lists beats one that is first in only one. No score scaling needed — only ranks.

Graph RAG

Analogy — a detective's wall Plain RAG hands the detective a pile of loose paragraphs. Graph RAG pins people, places and events on a wall and connects them with string — so "how is X linked to Y?" can be answered by following the string.
  • Idea: turn text into a knowledge graph — an LLM extracts entities (nodes) and relationships (edges) from documents. Retrieval then follows graph connections instead of (or as well as) vector similarity.
  • Microsoft's GraphRAG pipeline (general-knowledge summary): extract entities/relations → cluster the graph into communities → have an LLM write a summary per community. Local questions use the neighbourhood of the entities mentioned; global questions ("main themes?") combine the community summaries.
  • Strength: multi-hop and "connect the dots" questions, and whole-corpus summaries where top-\( k \) chunk retrieval fails. Cost: expensive indexing (many LLM calls), graph quality depends on extraction, harder to keep up to date.
  • Another style queries an existing graph database by generating a graph query (text-to-Cypher) — the same idea as text-to-SQL.

Agentic RAG

The book's agent-based RAG taken further. An LLM agent controls retrieval as one of several tools:

  • Decides whether to retrieve at all, and from which source (vector store, web search, SQL database, graph).
  • Rewrites or decomposes the query into sub-questions.
  • Checks what came back ("is this relevant? enough?") and loops — retrieve again, try another source, or ask the user.
  • May use several specialised agents (router, retriever, grader, answerer). Built on the Reason → Act → Observe loop (ReAct) that you will study in Session 4.

Evaluating a RAG system

  • Retriever: precision/recall at \( k \), MAP (above), MRR, nDCG — did the right passage come back, and how high?
  • Generator / end-to-end: faithfulness (is every claim supported by the retrieved text?), answer relevance, context precision/recall (frameworks such as RAGAS use these), plus exact match / token F1 on QA datasets (Session 4) and LLM-as-judge for subjective output.

One-page summary

Memorise — RAG on one card RAG = retrieve top-\( k \) passages → put them in the prompt → LLM answers (open-book). Why: fewer hallucinations, private data, fresh knowledge. Sparse: tf = \( 1+\log_{10}\text{count} \), idf = \( \log_{10}(N/\text{df}) \), cosine score; BM25 adds saturation + length norm (\( k=1.2\text{–}2,\ b=0.75 \)). Inverted index = term → postings. Dense: cross-encoder (best, slow) · bi-encoder (fast) · ColBERT (MaxSim); Faiss for nearest-neighbour. Evaluate: P@k, R@k, interpolated precision, AP (mean of precision at relevant ranks), MAP. Extras: reranker, multi-hop, agent-based RAG, citations. Beyond the book: hybrid + RRF, Graph RAG (knowledge graph + community summaries), Agentic RAG (agent controls the loop).

Practice questions

Q1. A collection has N = 1,000 documents. The term "retrieval" occurs in 10 of them, and 100 times in document d. Compute tf, idf and tf-idf of "retrieval" in d.
tf = 1 + log₁₀(100) = 3. idf = log₁₀(1000/10) = 2. tf-idf = 3 × 2 = 6.
Q2. Why is the idf of a word that appears in every document exactly 0, and what does that mean for ranking?
idf = log₁₀(N/N) = log₁₀ 1 = 0. The word cannot discriminate between documents, so its tf-idf weight is 0 and it contributes nothing to any score (e.g. "good", "sweet" in Shakespeare).
Q3. A system returns 5 documents, judged R N R N R. The collection has 4 relevant documents in total. Give precision@5, recall@5 and AP (average over the returned relevant documents).
Relevant in top 5 = 3 → precision@5 = 3/5 = 0.60; recall@5 = 3/4 = 0.75. Precision at relevant ranks 1, 3, 5 = 1, 2/3, 3/5 → AP = (1 + 0.667 + 0.6)/3 = 0.756.
Q4. Using the book's definition, what is the interpolated precision at recall 0.5 in Fig. 11.7?
The maximum precision at any recall ≥ 0.5. Precisions at recall ≥ 0.5 are 0.63 (rank 8), 0.55, 0.55, 0.50…, 0.55 (rank 11), 0.47, 0.44, 0.36 → maximum = 0.63 (book Fig. 11.9).
Q5. Why can't a cross-encoder be used to search a million documents, and how do real systems still use it?
It must run BERT on the query paired with every document at query time. Real systems use a cheap first stage (BM25 or a bi-encoder) to get the top-N, then rerank only those N with the cross-encoder.
Q6. BM25 with k = 0 — what does the score reduce to?
The term-frequency factor becomes tf/(0+tf) = 1 for any term that occurs, so the score is just the sum of idf over query terms present in the document — no term-frequency effect (binary matching plus idf).
Q7. A 2,400-token document, chunk size 500, overlap 100. How many chunks?
Stride = 400. ⌈(2400 − 500)/400⌉ + 1 = ⌈4.75⌉ + 1 = 6 chunks (starts at 0, 400, 800, 1200, 1600, 2000; the last chunk is partial).
Q8. Give one question type where plain RAG struggles and Graph RAG helps.
Whole-corpus or relationship questions, e.g. "What are the main themes across all these reports?" or "How is person A connected to company B?". Top-k chunk similarity returns a few local passages; a knowledge graph with community summaries can aggregate and follow links.
