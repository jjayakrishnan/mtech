What Session 1 actually covered

Session 1 of NLP Applications is, in Prof. Chetana Gavankar's own words, "the trailer for the course" — logistics, the shape of the semester, and a tour of every application you'll build, with nothing technically hard yet. This page keeps that structure: the course map, how grading works, a walk through all 10 application areas from easiest to hardest, and then the one prerequisite gap the professor flagged out loud in the recording — contextual word embeddings and attention/Transformers — with a link to the primer that fills it in.

Good to know before you startThis course is intentionally practical

"NLP Applications" is the hands-on, build-things sibling of the NLP course — you already studied the theory (word embeddings, language models, parsing). This course is about applying it: programming assignments carry real weight (25%+10% situated learning), and every application is taught as "here's the simple statistical way, here's the LLM/Agentic AI way, and here's when you'd actually choose each one in a real company" rather than pure theory.

10 applications, easiest to hardest

The professor deliberately orders applications by difficulty — not every NLP problem needs an LLM, and knowing when a simple classical method beats an expensive Agentic AI pipeline is itself an exam-relevant skill ("why are we learning statistical methods when everyone uses Gen AI?" — because production systems use a hybrid approach, and the simple method is often cheaper, faster, and just as accurate).

EasiestSpell/grammar check Language detectionSentiment analysis SummarizationNER / info extraction Machine translationQA / Conversational AI RAG → Agentic RAGScalable & efficient systems

1 · Spell & grammar check — the baselineAlmost 100% accuracy, minimal cost

Classic Naive Bayes-style statistical methods already nail this — no "fancy Agentic AI" required, in the professor's own words. Still a real production component, though: it's the first filter in every advanced pipeline downstream (conversational AI, QA, RAG all assume clean input text).

2 · Language detection & code-switchingA prerequisite step, not a final answer

You detect the language before you can translate it — you can't pick "English→Tamil" vs "English→French" without first knowing what you're starting from. Gets genuinely hard with code-switching: a sentence that mixes two languages in one breath, e.g. a WhatsApp message like "Bro, I'm so kashtapadardhu (struggling) with this assignment" — common among bilingual speakers, and classic single-language detectors choke on it.

AnalogyImagine Shazam, but instead of identifying a song it has to identify which language(s) are playing — and sometimes two songs are mixed together in one track (code-switching) and it has to say which parts belong to which.

3 · Sentiment analysisPositive, negative — or something more nuanced

Classifying text as positive/negative for a movie review, product review, or political statement.

AnalogyYour dog's tail wagging fast and loose is a positive-sentiment signal; a low, slow wag with ears back is negative — you're reading an emotional signal from behavioral cues, the same way a sentiment model reads emotional valence from word choice, punctuation ("!!!"), and emoji.

4 · Summarization — extractive vs. abstractiveTwo very different skills

Extractive summarization picks out actual sentences from the source, verbatim. Abstractive summarization writes genuinely new sentences that capture the gist.

AnalogyA movie trailer built entirely from real clips spliced from the film is extractive. A friend telling you "basically, the hero loses everything, then wins it back in the end" in their own words, having watched the whole thing, is abstractive — much harder, because it requires actually understanding the content, not just picking good excerpts.

5 · Information extraction — NER, relations, eventsTurning text into structured facts

Named Entity Recognition (NER) finds proper nouns (people, places, organizations) in text — and leans on part-of-speech tagging, since named entities typically start with proper nouns. Relation extraction then connects two entities ("Google acquired DeepMind" → acquired(Google, DeepMind)), and event extraction adds the "when".

AnalogyExactly like IMDb auto-tagging a movie review with the cast, director, and studio it mentions — extracting the "who" (entities), then the "did what to whom" (relations), then "when did it happen" (events).

6 · Machine translationStatistical → neural → Indic-language focus

A headline application this run — explicitly requested by industry contacts, and tied to national initiatives like AI4Bharat (IIT Madras) and Sarvam AI building Indian-language models. You'll cover statistical MT first (Session 9), then neural MT (Session 10), then Indic languages specifically (Session 11).

AnalogyDubbing a foreign film: the dubbing studio first has to know the source language, then produce a target-language script that preserves meaning (not a word-for-word substitution — idioms, humor, and grammar all shift), which is exactly why neural MT (which models whole-sentence meaning) beats older word-by-word statistical MT.

7 · Question answering & conversational AIWhere the real tooling lives

A huge open-source and commercial tooling ecosystem exists here (Hugging Face, transformers, ready datasets). The course builds these as hybrid systems — not pure LLM, but LLM + RAG + Agentic AI techniques working together, since a bare LLM alone hallucinates on specific factual questions.

8 · RAG — Naïve, Graph, and AgenticThe hardest application area in the course

You met plain RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation) in your NLP course. Here you go deeper: Graph RAG (retrieval over a knowledge graph of connected facts, not just flat text chunks) and Agentic RAG (the system decides when and what to retrieve, possibly retrieving multiple times, rather than a fixed retrieve-then-generate pipeline).

AnalogyNaive RAG is a friend who looks up one relevant fact before answering your question. Graph RAG is a friend who also knows how facts connect to each other — they can answer "how is X related to Y" because they're navigating a web of relationships, not just isolated snippets. Agentic RAG is that same friend deciding on their own, mid-conversation, "actually, I should go look that up again before I answer this follow-up" — autonomous, iterative retrieval instead of one fixed lookup.

9 · Agentic AI & guardrailsPowerful, expensive, and needs a leash

Agentic AI systems involve real cost, so production teams don't reach for them by default — they start simple and escalate only where needed. Whenever agents are used, guardrails and a human-in-the-loop are treated as non-negotiable.

AnalogyA guide dog is trained for genuine autonomy — it can refuse a "forward" command if a car is coming — but it still works within strict boundaries set by its training and handler. Guardrails on an AI agent are the same idea: real autonomy, inside limits that are enforced, not optional.

10 · Scalable & efficient NLP systemsNew this run — the "it worked in my notebook" trap

Added to the syllabus specifically because something that works beautifully in a proof-of-concept can fall apart in production: latency issues, scalability issues, efficiency issues that never showed up on a small test set. Session 15 covers model compression and inference optimization directly.

Prerequisites you need

From the recording: when the professor asked whether everyone had taken the NLP prerequisite course, one student said they hadn't — and the professor's advice was direct: "you might have to brush up a little bit… for some of the things like contextual word embedding", and later, more broadly: "you have to have the knowledge of attention mechanism, transformers."

If any of this is unfamiliar Read the shared Prerequisites Primer before Session 2 — it covers word embeddings, why plain RNNs struggle, attention (with a worked example), multi-head attention, and encoder vs. decoder vs. encoder-decoder (directly relevant here: machine translation and abstractive summarization are encoder-decoder tasks; sentiment analysis and NER lean encoder-only).

Self-check

Q1. A startup wants to flag spam comments on their blog. Should they reach for an LLM/Agentic AI pipeline, or a classical ML classifier? Why?
A classical classifier (e.g. Naive Bayes on bag-of-words features) — spam detection is explicitly called out as the easiest NLP application, already near 100% accuracy with minimal cost using traditional ML. Reaching for an expensive Agentic AI pipeline here would be over-engineering: more latency, more cost, no meaningful accuracy gain.
Q2. Why does language detection have to run before machine translation in a pipeline, rather than after?
Translation requires knowing the source language to select the correct source→target model/pipeline (e.g. English→Tamil vs. French→Tamil use different models). You can't choose the right translation path without first knowing what you're translating from — language detection is a prerequisite step, not a parallel or downstream one.
Q3. What's the practical difference between Naive RAG and Agentic RAG, in one sentence?
Naive RAG retrieves once, in a fixed pipeline (retrieve → generate); Agentic RAG lets the system decide, autonomously and possibly repeatedly, whether and what to retrieve based on how the answer is unfolding — iterative, self-directed retrieval rather than one fixed lookup.
