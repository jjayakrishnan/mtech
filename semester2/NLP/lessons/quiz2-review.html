Dependency Parsing

Transition-Based: Arc-Standard vs Arc-Eager

Arc-Standard

  • Conservative — waits until all right-dependents of a head are processed before attaching
  • Every arc is "complete" when created
  • Bottom-up construction
  • Cannot create an arc for a head that still has pending right-children on the buffer

Arc-Eager

  • Incremental arc creation — attaches as soon as head-dependent relationship is detected
  • A head can receive new right-dependents after it has already been attached
  • Generally faster; processes most sentences in fewer transitions
  • Can make "wrong" early commitments → error propagation

Graph-Based Parsing

Instead of a left-to-right transition sequence, graph-based parsers:

  1. Score every possible arc between every pair of words
  2. Find the spanning tree that maximises the total arc score
Algorithm

The Chu-Liu/Edmonds algorithm finds the Maximum Spanning Tree (MST) of a directed graph in O(n²). This is the canonical algorithm for graph-based dependency parsing.

Exam Trap

CKY and Earley's are constituency parsers (they operate on phrase-structure CFGs). They are not used for dependency parsing. MST is the answer whenever the question involves scoring all arcs globally.

Constituency Parsing & CKY

Chomsky Normal Form (CNF)

CKY requires the grammar to be in CNF — every rule is either:

  • \( A \rightarrow B\, C \) (two non-terminals)
  • \( A \rightarrow w \) (one terminal)

Any CFG can be converted to CNF without changing the language it generates.

CKY Chart Size

Formula

For a sentence of n words, the CKY chart is a triangular table. The number of cells to fill is:

$$\text{cells} = \frac{n(n+1)}{2}$$

For n = 12: \(\dfrac{12 \times 13}{2} = 78\) cells.

Analogy

Think of the chart as the upper-triangle of an n×n matrix. Cell (i,j) holds every non-terminal that can span words i through j. The diagonal is all the single-word spans; the top cell (0, n) is the full sentence.

Complexity

CKY runs in \( O(n^3 |G|) \) where |G| is the grammar size. The question said "ignoring grammar size" — that's why only the n-dependent term matters.

HMM & Viterbi Decoding

The HMM for POS Tagging

A Hidden Markov Model treats POS tags as hidden states and words as observations. Two key probability tables:

ParameterFormulaMeaning
Transition\( P(t_i \mid t_{i-1}) \)How likely is tag t_i to follow tag t_{i-1}?
Emission\( P(w_i \mid t_i) \)How likely is word w_i to be produced by tag t_i?

Viterbi Algorithm

Objective

Find the tag sequence \(\hat{T}\) that maximises the joint probability of the whole sequence:

$$\hat{T} = \arg\max_T \prod_{i=1}^{n} P(t_i \mid t_{i-1}) \cdot P(w_i \mid t_i)$$

Viterbi is dynamic programming over the trellis — it keeps only the best predecessor for each state at each position, rather than enumerating all \( |T|^n \) sequences.

Exam Trap

"Maximises local emission probabilities only" (option a in Q8) is greedy tag-by-tag — it ignores the transition term and can produce grammatically impossible tag sequences. Viterbi always uses both transition and emission.

Why Domain Shift Hurts POS Accuracy

A tagger trained on news text learns emission probabilities like P(NN | "bank") and transition probabilities from formal grammar. Social media text introduces:

  • OOV (out-of-vocabulary) words: hashtags, abbreviations, emojis
  • Non-standard capitalisation and punctuation
  • Different grammar: sentence fragments, no subject, etc.

Both the emission table and the transition table become unreliable — accuracy drops sharply.

Transformers & Self-Attention

Self-Attention at a Glance

Scaled Dot-Product Attention
$$\text{Attention}(Q,K,V) = \text{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^T}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V$$

Each token attends to every other token simultaneously — no sequential dependency. This is what enables long-range context capture.

Why Multiple Heads?

Think of each head as wearing different "glasses": one head might focus on syntactic subject-verb relations, another on coreference (who "he" refers to), another on semantic similarity. Concatenating all heads gives a richer representation than any single head could produce.

Encoder Output Count

Key Fact

The Transformer encoder produces exactly one contextualized vector per input token. For 12 input words → 12 output vectors. Each vector encodes that word's meaning in the full sentence context.

Contrast with seq2seq RNNs: the encoder collapses the whole sentence into a single fixed-length "bottleneck" vector — which is why attention was invented in the first place.

Why Transformers Handle Ambiguity Better

SentenceAmbiguous wordHow Transformer resolves it

"I saw her duck."duck (bird vs action)Attends to "saw" (perception verb) + "her" (object pronoun → action more natural)

Contextual Embeddings

Static Embeddings (Word2Vec, GloVe)

  • One fixed vector per word type
  • "duck" always has the same vector regardless of sentence
  • Cannot resolve polysemy or syntactic ambiguity
  • Fast; no context needed at inference time

Contextual Embeddings (BERT, GPT)

  • Vector depends on the surrounding sentence
  • "duck" in "her duck" gets different vector than "duck down"
  • Resolves polysemy, POS ambiguity, coreference
  • Built on Transformer self-attention
The Key Insight

"I saw her duck" is structurally ambiguous — "duck" can be a noun (object of "saw") or a verb (complement of "saw her"). The contextual embedding of "duck" encodes cues from the full sentence: "saw" as a perception verb, "her" as an object pronoun, and the absence of a second object after "duck" (if noun). The embedding reflects which reading is more probable in context.

Probabilistic CFG (PCFG)

A PCFG attaches a probability to each grammar rule. The probability of a parse tree is the product of the probabilities of all rules used:

Parse Tree Probability
$$P(\text{tree}) = \prod_{\text{rules used}} P(A \rightarrow \beta)$$

When multiple parse trees exist for an ambiguous sentence, a PCFG parser selects the most probable tree:

Example from Q9

Tree A = 0.00048, Tree B = 0.00062.
Since 0.00062 > 0.00048, the parser chooses Tree B. Simplicity, depth, or number of nodes are irrelevant — only probability counts.

Relationship to CKY

CKY can be extended to PCFGs: instead of just filling cells with possible non-terminals, fill with the most probable non-terminal derivation for each span. This is the probabilistic CKY algorithm — it runs in the same O(n³|G|) time.

Common Exam Traps

TrapWhat to Remember
CKY cell count n(n+1)/2, NOT n² or n. For n=12: 78 cells, not 144 or 12.
MST vs CKY for dependency parsing CKY and Earley's are for constituency (phrase-structure) parsing. MST (Chu-Liu/Edmonds) is for graph-based dependency parsing.
Viterbi = local greedy Viterbi is NOT greedy. It uses dynamic programming to find the globally optimal tag sequence — not the locally best tag at each step.
Arc-Eager = bottom-up Arc-Eager is incremental, not bottom-up. "Incremental" means it attaches arcs immediately without waiting — this is the key distinguishing property.
Transformer encoder output count Always one vector per token. For 12 words → 12 vectors. The number of attention heads does NOT change this — heads affect the internal representation, not the output count.
Multiple heads = bigger vocabulary Multiple heads learn different relationship types, not more vocabulary. Vocabulary size is set by the tokenizer, not by the number of attention heads.
PCFG prefers simpler trees PCFGs are purely probabilistic. They have no notion of simplicity or parsimony — the highest-probability tree wins, regardless of depth or rule count.
Transfer learning improves accuracy Transfer learning (fine-tuning a pretrained model) and contextual embeddings generally improve POS accuracy. Domain shift (mismatch between training and test domain) reduces it.

Self-Test Drill

Try each question before opening the answer.

Dependency Parsing

  1. What is the key difference between Arc-Eager and Arc-Standard?
    Show answer

    Arc-Eager uses incremental arc creation — it attaches a head to its dependent as soon as the relationship is detected, without waiting for all dependents of that head to be processed. Arc-Standard waits until all right-dependents are seen before reducing.

  2. A graph-based parser scores all possible arcs and needs to find the best tree. Which algorithm does it use?
    Show answer

    Chu-Liu/Edmonds algorithm (Maximum Spanning Tree). It finds the directed spanning tree with the highest total arc score in O(n²).

CKY & Constituency Parsing

  1. A sentence has 8 words. The grammar is in CNF. How many cells does the CKY chart have?
    Show answer

    \(\dfrac{8 \times 9}{2} = 36\) cells.

  2. Why must the grammar be in CNF before applying CKY?
    Show answer

    CKY works by combining pairs of sub-spans. For this to work, every rule must produce exactly two non-terminals (A → B C) or one terminal (A → w). Rules with more than two symbols on the right side cannot be directly handled by the chart — CNF guarantees the binary structure CKY requires.

HMM & Viterbi

  1. What two probability tables does the Viterbi algorithm combine?
    Show answer

    Transition probabilities P(t_i | t_{i-1}) and emission probabilities P(w_i | t_i). Both must be used — using only emissions is greedy and ignores grammatical constraints.

  2. A POS tagger trained on news articles is applied to tweets. What happens to accuracy, and why?
    Show answer

    Accuracy drops due to domain shift. Social media text has OOV words (hashtags, slang), non-standard grammar, and different word distributions. Both the emission table (P(word|tag)) and transition table (P(tag|prev tag)) become unreliable outside the training domain.

Transformers & Embeddings

  1. A Transformer encoder processes a sentence of 15 tokens. How many output vectors does it produce, and why?
    Show answer

    15 — one contextualized vector per input token. Each vector encodes the token's meaning in the context of the full sentence via self-attention.

  2. Why does "bank" get a different embedding in "river bank" vs "bank loan" in BERT but the same embedding in Word2Vec?
    Show answer

    Word2Vec assigns one static vector per word type, trained on co-occurrence statistics across all contexts. BERT uses Transformer self-attention: the vector for "bank" is computed from the entire input sentence, so different surrounding words (river vs loan) produce different output vectors — capturing the different meanings.

PCFG

  1. Tree X has probability 0.0031, Tree Y has probability 0.0019. Which does a PCFG parser choose?
    Show answer

    Tree X. PCFG always selects the parse with the highest probability: 0.0031 > 0.0019.
