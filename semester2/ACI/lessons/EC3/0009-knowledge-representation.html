1. Knowledge-Based Agents & the Wumpus World

A Knowledge-Based Agent does not just react to percepts — it reasons about the world. At its core is a Knowledge Base (KB): a set of sentences written in a formal language that represent what the agent knows.

The Three KB Operations

  • TELL(KB, sentence) — Add a new fact to the KB (from a percept or a rule)
  • ASK(KB, query) — Query whether a sentence is entailed by the KB
  • MAKE-PERCEPT-SENTENCE / MAKE-ACTION-QUERY — Build sentences from raw percepts/actions

Every cycle: TELL the percept → ASK for the best action → TELL the chosen action back to KB.

The Wumpus World

A 4×4 grid maze, used throughout the course as the canonical KB-agent testbed. The agent starts at [1,1] facing right and must find the gold without falling into a pit or being eaten by the Wumpus.

2. Propositional Logic — Syntax

Propositional Logic (PL) is the simplest formal logic. Sentences are built from atomic propositions (atoms) connected by logical connectives.

Syntax Building Blocks

  • Atoms: \( P, Q, R, \ldots \) — represent basic facts (e.g. \( B_{11} \) = "Breeze in [1,1]")
  • Constants: True (always true), False (always false)
  • Complex sentences: atoms + connectives

The Five Connectives

SymbolNameRead asExample
\( \neg \)Negation (NOT)"It is not the case that…"\( \neg B_{11} \) = "No breeze at [1,1]"
\( \land \)Conjunction (AND)"… and …"\( P \land Q \) = "P and Q"
\( \lor \)Disjunction (OR)"… or … (inclusive)"\( P \lor Q \)
\( \Rightarrow \)Implication (IF–THEN)"If … then …"\( B_{11} \Rightarrow P_{12} \lor P_{21} \)
\( \Leftrightarrow \)Biconditional (IFF)"… if and only if …"\( B_{11} \Leftrightarrow (P_{12} \lor P_{21}) \)

Precedence (highest → lowest): \( \neg \) > \( \land \) > \( \lor \) > \( \Rightarrow \) > \( \Leftrightarrow \)

Common Mistake: Implication Truth

\( P \Rightarrow Q \) is false only when P is true and Q is false. When P is false, the implication is vacuously true — regardless of Q. Students often think a false premise makes the implication false.

3. Truth Tables & Logical Equivalences

A model is an assignment of true/false to every atom. A sentence is satisfiable if there exists at least one model making it true; valid (tautology) if true in all models; unsatisfiable if true in no model.

Implication Elim.\( P \Rightarrow Q \equiv \neg P \lor Q \)

Biconditional Elim.\( P \Leftrightarrow Q \equiv (P \Rightarrow Q) \land (Q \Rightarrow P) \)

Distributivity\( P \land (Q \lor R) \equiv (P \land Q) \lor (P \land R) \)

4. Entailment & TT-Entailment

Entailment Defined

\( KB \models \alpha \) means "the KB entails \( \alpha \)" — \( \alpha \) is true in every model in which the KB is true.

Analogy: if you know "All humans are mortal" and "Socrates is human", you can entail "Socrates is mortal" — not just guess it.

TT-Entailment Algorithm (Truth Table Enumeration)

To check \( KB \models \alpha \): enumerate all \( 2^n \) models (n = number of atoms). For each model where KB is true, check if \( \alpha \) is also true. If yes for all such models → entailed.

Worked example: KB = \( \{P \lor Q, \; \neg Q\} \). Does KB entail P?

PQ\( P \lor Q \)\( \neg Q \)KB true?P true?
TTTFF—
TFTTTT ✓
FTTFF—
FFFTF—

Only one model makes KB true (row 2), and P is true there. So KB ⊨ P. ✓

TT-Entailment Limitation

TT-Entailment is complete (always gives the right answer) but intractable — \( O(2^n) \) models grow exponentially. For a KB with 100 atoms, that's \( 2^{100} \approx 10^{30} \) rows. Resolution (§6) solves this efficiently.

5. Conjunctive Normal Form (CNF)

CNF = a conjunction (AND) of clauses, where each clause is a disjunction (OR) of literals (atoms or their negations).

Example in CNF: \( (P \lor Q) \land (\neg P \lor R) \land (\neg Q \lor \neg R) \)

Why CNF? Resolution Only Works on CNF

Every PL sentence can be converted to CNF. Once in CNF, the Resolution algorithm can test entailment efficiently (§6).

4-Step CNF Conversion Algorithm

  1. Eliminate \( \Leftrightarrow \): \( A \Leftrightarrow B \rightarrow (A \Rightarrow B) \land (B \Rightarrow A) \)
  2. Eliminate \( \Rightarrow \): \( A \Rightarrow B \rightarrow \neg A \lor B \)
  3. Move \( \neg \) inward (De Morgan's): push all negations to atoms only
    • \( \neg(A \land B) \rightarrow \neg A \lor \neg B \)
    • \( \neg(A \lor B) \rightarrow \neg A \land \neg B \)
    • \( \neg\neg A \rightarrow A \)
  4. Distribute \( \lor \) over \( \land \): \( A \lor (B \land C) \rightarrow (A \lor B) \land (A \lor C) \)

Worked Trace: Convert \( (P \Leftrightarrow Q) \) to CNF

StepCurrent FormRule Applied
Start\( P \Leftrightarrow Q \)—
Step 1\( (P \Rightarrow Q) \land (Q \Rightarrow P) \)Eliminate \( \Leftrightarrow \)
Step 2\( (\neg P \lor Q) \land (\neg Q \lor P) \)Eliminate \( \Rightarrow \) (twice)
Done\( (\neg P \lor Q) \land (\neg Q \lor P) \)Already in CNF (two clauses)

Exam Trap: Step 4 Distributivity

After moving \( \neg \) inward, you may end up with \( A \lor (B \land C) \). You must distribute to get \( (A \lor B) \land (A \lor C) \). Forgetting this step leaves you with mixed AND/OR inside a clause — which is not CNF. This is the #1 error in CNF conversion.

🔑 CNF Quick Check

A formula is in CNF when:

  • The outermost connective is only \( \land \) (or a single clause)
  • Each clause contains only \( \lor \) and literals (no \( \Rightarrow \), no \( \Leftrightarrow \), no nested \( \land \) inside a clause)
  • Negations appear only directly before atoms (not before parentheses)

6. Resolution & Proof by Refutation

The Resolution Rule

Resolution Rule (PL)

Given two clauses containing complementary literals \( \ell \) and \( \neg\ell \):

$$\frac{P \lor \ell \quad\quad \neg\ell \lor Q}{P \lor Q}$$

The resolvent \( P \lor Q \) is produced by cancelling \( \ell \) and \( \neg\ell \).

Unit Resolution: if one clause is a single literal \( \ell \) and the other is \( \neg\ell \lor Q \), the resolvent is \( Q \).

Proof by Refutation (Resolution Theorem Proving)

To prove \( KB \models \alpha \):

  1. Add \( \neg\alpha \) to KB
  2. Convert every sentence in KB to CNF
  3. Repeatedly apply the resolution rule to pairs of clauses
  4. If the empty clause \( \square \) is derived → contradiction → KB ⊨ α proved ✓
  5. If no new clauses can be derived → KB does not entail α

Worked Refutation Trace

KB = \( \{P \lor Q, \; P \Rightarrow R, \; Q \Rightarrow R\} \). Prove \( R \).

Step 0: Add \( \neg R \) to KB. Convert to CNF:

  • Clause 1: \( P \lor Q \)
  • Clause 2: \( \neg P \lor R \) (from \( P \Rightarrow R \))
  • Clause 3: \( \neg Q \lor R \) (from \( Q \Rightarrow R \))
  • Clause 4: \( \neg R \) (the negated goal)
StepClause 1Clause 2Resolvent
1\( \neg P \lor R \)\( \neg R \)\( \neg P \) (unit resolution)
2\( \neg Q \lor R \)\( \neg R \)\( \neg Q \)
3\( P \lor Q \)\( \neg P \)\( Q \)
4\( Q \)\( \neg Q \)\( \square \) — Empty clause! R proved.

🔑 Resolution Key Points

  • Resolution is sound (every derived sentence is true) and complete (if KB ⊨ α, resolution will find it)
  • Empty clause \( \square \) = contradiction = proof succeeds
  • You can only resolve one pair of complementary literals per resolution step
  • The resolution rule requires both input clauses to be in CNF

7. First-Order Logic (FOL / Predicate Logic)

Propositional logic can't express "All kings are people" compactly — you'd need one atom per king. FOL introduces objects, properties, and relations, letting us write universal and existential statements.

FOL Building Blocks

ConceptDefinitionExamples
ConstantsSpecific named objects\( John, \; Mary, \; 2, \; BITS \)
VariablesPlaceholders for objects\( x, \; y, \; z \)
FunctionsMap objects to objects\( MotherOf(x), \; Plus(x,y) \)
PredicatesMap objects to true/false\( King(x), \; Loves(x,y), \; GreaterThan(x,y) \)
TermsAny constant, variable, or \( f(\text{terms}) \)\( x, \; John, \; MotherOf(John) \)
SentencesPredicate applied to terms, or sentences + connectives + quantifiers\( King(John), \; \forall x \; King(x) \Rightarrow Person(x) \)

Universal & Existential Quantifiers

QuantifierSymbolRead asExample
Universal\( \forall x \)"For all x …"\( \forall x \; King(x) \Rightarrow Person(x) \)
"All kings are people"
Existential\( \exists x \)"There exists an x such that …"\( \exists x \; Crown(x) \land OnHead(x, John) \)
"There is a crown on John's head"

English → FOL: Worked Examples (from 19 Jul transcript)

EnglishFOL
All students who attend all classes pass the exam\( \forall s \; Student(s) \land (\forall c \; Attends(s,c)) \Rightarrow Passes(s) \)
Some person knows every other person\( \exists x \; Person(x) \land (\forall y \; Person(y) \Rightarrow Knows(x,y)) \)
No student failed both exams\( \neg \exists s \; Student(s) \land Failed(s, EC2) \land Failed(s, EC3) \)
Every room either has a pit or is adjacent to gold\( \forall r \; Pit(r) \lor (\exists r' \; Adjacent(r,r') \land Gold(r')) \)

🔑 Quantifier De Morgan Laws

OriginalEquivalent
\( \neg \forall x \; P(x) \)\( \exists x \; \neg P(x) \) — "Not everyone P" = "Someone is not P"
\( \neg \exists x \; P(x) \)\( \forall x \; \neg P(x) \) — "Nobody P" = "Everyone is not P"

Scope rule: \( \forall x \; P(x) \Rightarrow Q(x) \) means "for each x, if P(x) then Q(x)" — x is bound inside the whole formula. Never split the quantifier scope carelessly.

Exam Trap: Quantifier + Connective Combination

  • \( \forall x \; P(x) \land Q(x) \) — both P and Q hold for every x ✓ (often what's intended)
  • \( \forall x \; P(x) \Rightarrow Q(x) \) — for every x, if P then Q (most natural for "all P are Q")
  • \( \exists x \; P(x) \land Q(x) \) — use \( \land \) with \( \exists \), not \( \Rightarrow \): "some x is both P and Q"
  • \( \exists x \; P(x) \Rightarrow Q(x) \) — this is vacuously true if any x fails P! Almost never what you want.

Rule: Universal quantifier typically pairs with \( \Rightarrow \). Existential quantifier typically pairs with \( \land \).

8. Forward & Backward Chaining

Both algorithms work on definite clause KBs — clauses with exactly one positive literal (i.e., rules of the form \( A \land B \Rightarrow C \), or facts like \( A \)).

Inference Rules

  • Modus Ponens: Given \( \alpha \Rightarrow \beta \) and \( \alpha \), derive \( \beta \)
  • Modus Tollens: Given \( \alpha \Rightarrow \beta \) and \( \neg\beta \), derive \( \neg\alpha \)

Forward Chaining — Data-Driven

Start from known facts. Apply rules whenever their premises are satisfied. Repeat until query is derived or no new facts can be added.

  • Analogy: a scientist observing data and systematically deriving conclusions
  • Complete for definite clauses: will find all derivable facts
  • May derive irrelevant facts (works toward all possible conclusions)
KB (definite clauses): Fact: A Fact: B Rule 1: A ∧ B → C Rule 2: C → D Query: D? Forward Chain: Iteration 1: A, B → apply Rule 1 → derive C Iteration 2: C → apply Rule 2 → derive D ✓ Query answered!

Backward Chaining — Goal-Driven

Start from the query/goal. Work backward to check if derivable from KB. Only follows paths relevant to the goal.

  • Analogy: a detective working backward from a conclusion to find evidence
  • More efficient than forward chaining when goal is specific
  • Can loop (need visited tracking)
Query: D? To prove D, need C (from Rule 2) To prove C, need A ∧ B (from Rule 1) A? — yes, it's a fact ✓ B? — yes, it's a fact ✓ → C proved → D proved ✓

✏️ Self-Check 3: FOL Translation — "Every student who submits Assignment 2 on time gets a bonus"

Solution

Identify predicates: \( Student(x) \), \( SubmittedOnTime(x, Assignment2) \), \( GetsBonus(x) \)

$$\forall x \; Student(x) \land SubmittedOnTime(x, A2) \Rightarrow GetsBonus(x)$$
