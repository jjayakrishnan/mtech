1. Multi-Agent Environments

So far agents have acted alone. In the real world, multiple agents share the same environment — robots on a warehouse floor, bidders at an auction, countries negotiating a treaty. Their decisions interact: your best move depends on what others do.

Two Broad Categories

TypeAgentsGoalTool
Non-CooperativeSelf-interested; each maximises own payoffPredict rational play without binding agreementsNash Equilibrium, Dominant Strategies
CooperativeCan form binding coalitions; share rewardsFair allocation of joint gainsCoalitions, Shapley Value

Normal-Form (Strategic) Game

The standard representation of a one-shot interaction:

  • Players: finite set \( N = \{1, 2, \ldots, n\} \)
  • Strategies: each player \( i \) chooses from strategy set \( S_i \)
  • Payoffs: \( u_i(s_1, s_2, \ldots, s_n) \) — utility for player i given the joint strategy profile

A strategy profile \( s = (s_1, \ldots, s_n) \) is one choice per player. The payoff matrix tabulates all profiles.

2. Non-Cooperative Game Theory

Non-cooperative game theory asks: given that all players are rational and self-interested, what will they do? We represent two-player games as a payoff matrix.

Reading a Payoff Matrix

Each cell shows (Row player's payoff, Column player's payoff).

Example — Matching Pennies:

Col: HeadsCol: Tails
Row: Heads(+1, −1)(−1, +1)
Row: Tails(−1, +1)(+1, −1)

Row player wins (+1) when both choose same; Column wins when they differ. This is a zero-sum game — one player's gain is the other's loss.

The Prisoner's Dilemma — Classic Example

Two suspects (Row = R, Col = C) each choose to Cooperate (stay silent) or Defect (betray). Payoffs represent years NOT in jail (higher = better).

C: CooperateC: Defect
R: Cooperate(3, 3)(0, 5)
R: Defect(5, 0)(1, 1) ← Nash

Both get 3 if they cooperate, but each is individually tempted to defect (getting 5). The rational outcome is (Defect, Defect) = (1,1) — both are worse off. This illustrates the tension between individual and collective rationality.

3. Dominant Strategies

Dominance Definitions

  • Strictly dominant strategy: strategy \( s_i \) that gives strictly higher payoff than any other strategy, regardless of what other players do.
  • Weakly dominant strategy: \( s_i \) gives payoff ≥ any other strategy for all opponent actions, and strictly better for at least one.
  • Dominated strategy: a strategy that is always outperformed by another — rational players never play dominated strategies.

Finding dominant strategies is the first step before computing Nash equilibrium.

Worked Example — Iterated Elimination of Dominated Strategies (IEDS)

Eliminate strategies that are dominated, reducing the matrix step by step.

Col: LeftCol: CentreCol: Right
Row: Up(3, 1)(2, 3)(1, 2)
Row: Down(1, 2)(4, 1)(3, 3)
StepObservationEliminated

2After removing Left, Row: Down gives (4,1) or (3,3) vs Up gives (2,3) or (1,2). Down ≥ Up in both remaining columnsRow: Up
3Remaining: Row=Down, Col=Centre or Right. Col prefers Right (3) over Centre (1) when Row=DownCol: Centre

4. Nash Equilibrium

Nash Equilibrium — Definition

A strategy profile \( s^* = (s_1^*, \ldots, s_n^*) \) is a Nash Equilibrium (NE) if no player can gain by unilaterally deviating:

$$u_i(s_i^*, s_{-i}^*) \geq u_i(s_i, s_{-i}^*) \quad \forall i, \; \forall s_i \in S_i$$

where \( s_{-i}^* \) denotes all players' strategies except player i.

Intuitively: at a NE, each player is playing their best response to what everyone else is doing. Nobody has an incentive to change.

Finding Nash Equilibria — Best Response Method

For each column (Col's strategy), underline Row's best payoff. For each row (Row's strategy), circle Col's best payoff. A cell where both Row's and Col's payoffs are marked = Nash Equilibrium.

Example — Battle of the Sexes:

Row = Alice, Col = Bob. Both prefer to be together but have different preferences (Alice prefers Opera, Bob prefers Football).

Bob: OperaBob: Football
Alice: Opera(2, 1) ← NE(0, 0)
Alice: Football(0, 0)(1, 2) ← NE

Two pure-strategy Nash Equilibria: (Opera, Opera) and (Football, Football). Neither player wants to deviate unilaterally from either — if Alice goes to Opera and Bob goes to Football, Alice gets 0; she prefers to follow Bob.

Nash Equilibrium — Prisoner's Dilemma Verification

Check (Defect, Defect): Can Row improve by switching to Cooperate? If Col=Defect: Row gets 1 (Defect) vs 0 (Cooperate) → No improvement. Similarly for Col. (Defect, Defect) is the unique NE.

Note: the NE (1,1) is Pareto-inferior to (Cooperate, Cooperate) = (3,3). This is the tragedy of non-cooperative games.

Exam Traps — Nash Equilibrium

  • Nash ≠ Pareto optimal: A Nash Equilibrium can be collectively suboptimal (Prisoner's Dilemma). Don't confuse "stable" with "efficient".
  • Multiple NE can exist (Battle of the Sexes has two). Always check all cells.
  • A dominant strategy profile is always a NE, but a NE need not involve dominant strategies.
  • Mixed-strategy NE: If no pure-strategy NE exists (e.g., Matching Pennies), one exists in mixed strategies — but EC3 typically focuses on pure-strategy NE.

🔑 Nash Equilibrium — Quick Check Method

  1. For each column, find Row's highest payoff (first number) — mark it ✓
  2. For each row, find Col's highest payoff (second number) — mark it ✓
  3. Any cell with both payoffs marked = Pure-strategy Nash Equilibrium

If no pure NE found → there is a mixed-strategy NE (not required for most EC3 questions).

5. Cooperative Game Theory

In cooperative games, players can form coalitions — binding agreements to act together and share the rewards. The key questions become: Which coalition will form? How should the joint payoff be divided fairly?

Coalitions & Characteristic Functions

  • Coalition: any subset \( S \subseteq N \) of players who agree to cooperate
  • Grand coalition: \( N \) — all players cooperate
  • Characteristic function: \( v(S) \) — the total value (payoff) that coalition S can guarantee for itself, regardless of what non-members do
  • Key property: \( v(\emptyset) = 0 \), and often \( v(N) \geq v(S) + v(N \setminus S) \) (superadditivity — working together is at least as good as working apart)

Example — Three Programmers (from Aug 23 session)

Agents: Alice (A), Bob (B), Carol (C). Coalition values:

Coalition Sv(S)Interpretation
\( \{A\} \)0Alice alone can't complete the project
\( \{B\} \)0Bob alone can't complete the project
\( \{C\} \)0Carol alone can't complete the project
\( \{A, B\} \)50A and B together can finish half the work
\( \{A, C\} \)50A and C together can finish half the work
\( \{B, C\} \)0B and C have overlapping skills — no gain
\( \{A, B, C\} \)100All three complete the full project

Question: The grand coalition earns 100. How should it be split fairly?

6. Shapley Value — Fair Reward Distribution

The Shapley value \( \phi_i(v) \) is the unique fair allocation satisfying four axioms (efficiency, symmetry, dummy, additivity). It answers: "On average, how much does player i contribute across all possible coalition formations?"

Shapley Value Formula

$$\phi_i(v) = \sum_{S \subseteq N \setminus \{i\}} \frac{|S|!\;(n - |S| - 1)!}{n!} \bigl[v(S \cup \{i\}) - v(S)\bigr]$$
  • \( S \) — coalition not containing player i (what exists before i joins)
  • \( v(S \cup \{i\}) - v(S) \) — marginal contribution of i to coalition S
  • \( \dfrac{|S|!\,(n-|S|-1)!}{n!} \) — weight (how likely this ordering is)

Shapley Value — Intuition via Permutations

Equivalently: imagine all \( n! \) orderings of the players. In each ordering, player i joins at some point. Their Shapley value is the average marginal contribution across all orderings.

For n=3 players {A, B, C}, there are 3! = 6 orderings: ABC, ACB, BAC, BCA, CAB, CBA For each ordering, note what coalition exists when player i joins: Ordering ABC: A joins {} (MC=v{A}-v{}), B joins {A} (MC=v{AB}-v{A}), C joins {AB} Ordering ACB: A joins {} , C joins {A}, B joins {AC} ...and so on for all 6.

Worked Computation — Three Programmers Shapley Values

For 3 players, 6 orderings. We compute each player's marginal contribution per ordering:

Interpretation: Alice gets 50 because she is a "connector" — B and C are only useful when A is present. Alice contributes 100 when she joins {B,C} (in two orderings) and 50 in the other four.

🔑 Shapley Value Key Properties

  • Efficiency: \( \sum_{i \in N} \phi_i(v) = v(N) \) — total value fully distributed
  • Symmetry: if i and j are interchangeable (same MCs everywhere), \( \phi_i = \phi_j \)
  • Dummy player: if i contributes nothing (adds 0 to every coalition), \( \phi_i = 0 \)
  • Additivity: for two games v and w, \( \phi_i(v+w) = \phi_i(v) + \phi_i(w) \)

Formula shortcut: with 3 players, each Shapley value = (sum of MCs across all 6 orderings) ÷ 6.

7. Collective Decision Making

Beyond dividing payoffs, multi-agent systems need mechanisms for groups to make joint decisions — choosing a common action, electing a representative, or allocating resources.

Key Collective Decision Strategies

StrategyDescriptionUse case
Majority VotingOption with most votes wins. Simple but can ignore preference intensity.Elections, committee decisions
Plurality VotingHighest vote count wins even without majority. Can elect unpopular candidates.First-past-the-post elections
Borda CountEach voter ranks options; points assigned by rank (last=0, …, first=n−1). Sum points.Award rankings, sports polls
Condorcet WinnerOption that beats every other option in pairwise comparisons. Doesn't always exist.Tournament scheduling
VetoAny agent can block an option. Ensures unanimous consent but can deadlock.UN Security Council

Exam Traps — Game Theory

  • Shapley values must sum to v(N). If your values don't sum to the grand coalition value, there's a calculation error — use this as a self-check.
  • Characteristic function v(S) vs payoff matrix: don't confuse the payoff matrix (non-cooperative) with the characteristic function (cooperative). They are different representations.
  • Nash ≠ optimal: Nash equilibria describe stable but not necessarily efficient outcomes.
  • Marginal contribution order matters: v(S∪{i}) − v(S) changes depending on what S is when i arrives. Compute each ordering separately.

8. Practice Problems

✏️ Self-Check 1: Find Nash Equilibria in this payoff matrix
Col: XCol: Y
Row: A(4, 3)(1, 2)
Row: B(2, 1)(3, 4)

Solution

Step 1 — Row's best responses: If Col=X: Row prefers A (4>2). If Col=Y: Row prefers B (3>1). Mark: (A,X) and (B,Y).

Step 2 — Col's best responses: If Row=A: Col prefers X (3>2). If Row=B: Col prefers Y (4>1). Mark: (A,X) and (B,Y).

Nash Equilibria: (A, X) = (4,3) and (B, Y) = (3,4) — both cells have both players' best responses marked. ✓

✏️ Self-Check 2: Shapley Value — 2 players {P, Q} with v({P})=2, v({Q})=3, v({P,Q})=10

Solution

n=2, so 2! = 2 orderings:

OrderingMC of PMC of Q
P, Qv{P}−v{} = 2−0 = 2v{P,Q}−v{P} = 10−2 = 8
Q, Pv{P,Q}−v{Q} = 10−3 = 7v{Q}−v{} = 3−0 = 3
Sum ÷ 2φ_P = (2+7)/2 = 4.5φ_Q = (8+3)/2 = 5.5

Check: 4.5 + 5.5 = 10 = v(N) ✓

✏️ Self-Check 3: Is (Cooperate, Cooperate) a Nash Equilibrium in Prisoner's Dilemma?

Solution — No

At (Cooperate, Cooperate), Row gets 3. If Row switches to Defect (while Col stays Cooperate), Row gets 5 > 3. So Row has an incentive to deviate → (Cooperate, Cooperate) is NOT a Nash Equilibrium.

The unique NE is (Defect, Defect) = (1,1). Even though (3,3) is Pareto-superior, neither player will unilaterally move there from (Defect, Defect) — they'd get 0 by switching alone.
