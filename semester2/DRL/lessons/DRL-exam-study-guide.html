Multi-Armed Bandit

  • No state — just actions + rewards

  • EWMA: \( Q(a) \leftarrow Q(a) + \alpha[r - Q(a)] \)

  • \(\alpha=1\) → only last reward matters

MDP Formulation

  • Value Iteration: \( V \leftarrow \max_a \) over actions

Monte Carlo Methods

  • Off-policy: importance sampling ratio \(\rho\)
  • Weighted IS: \(\rho \cdot G / \sum \rho\)

Policy Gradient

3. Key Formulas — Cheat Sheet

UCB Action Selection (MAB)
$$ a^* = \arg\max_a \left[ Q(a) + c \cdot \sqrt{\frac{\ln t}{N(a)}} \right] $$ \(t\) = total time steps so far  ·  \(N(a)\) = times \(a\) was chosen
Exponential Recency-Weighted Average (EWMA)
$$ Q_{n+1}(a) = Q_n(a) + \alpha \left[ R_n - Q_n(a) \right] $$ \(\alpha \in (0,1]\)  ·  \(\alpha = 0.5\) common in exams
Bellman Expectation (Policy Evaluation)
$$ V_\pi(s) = \sum_a \pi(a|s) \sum_{s'} P(s'|s,a)\left[ R(s,a,s') + \gamma V_\pi(s') \right] $$
Bellman Optimality (Value Iteration)
$$ V^*(s) = \max_a \sum_{s'} P(s'|s,a)\left[ R + \gamma V^*(s') \right] $$
Discounted Return (MC)
$$ G_t = R_{t+1} + \gamma R_{t+2} + \gamma^2 R_{t+3} + \cdots $$ Compute backwards from terminal state
First-Visit MC Update
$$ V(s) \leftarrow V(s) + \frac{1}{n}\left[ G - V(s) \right] $$ \(n\) = number of times \(s\) has been visited so far
Importance Sampling Ratio
$$ \rho = \prod_t \frac{\pi(a_t|s_t)}{b(a_t|s_t)} $$ \(\pi\) = target policy  ·  \(b\) = behaviour policy
Policy Gradient Update (REINFORCE)
$$ \theta \leftarrow \theta + \alpha (G - b)\, \nabla_\theta \ln \pi_\theta(a|s) $$ \(b\) = baseline (often \(V(s)\) or a constant)
Linear Softmax Policy
$$ \pi(a|s) = \frac{\exp(\theta^\top \phi(s,a))}{\sum_{a'} \exp(\theta^\top \phi(s,a'))} $$ \(\phi(s)\) = feature vector of state \(s\)
UCB for MCTS (Tree)
$$ \text{UCB}(s,a) = Q(s,a) + c\sqrt{\frac{\ln N(s)}{N(s,a)}} $$ \(N(s)\) = parent visits  ·  \(N(s,a)\) = child visits
DQN Loss
$$ \mathcal{L}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}\!\left[\left(r + \gamma \max_{a'} Q_{\theta^-}(s',a') - Q_\theta(s,a)\right)^2\right] $$ \(\theta^-\) = frozen target network weights
Behavior Cloning Loss
$$ \mathcal{L}_{\text{BC}}(\theta) = -\frac{1}{N}\sum_i \log \pi_\theta(a_i^E \mid s_i) $$ \(a^E\) = expert action at state \(s\)
TD(0) Update & TD Error
$$ V(S_t) \leftarrow V(S_t) + \alpha\big[\underbrace{R_{t+1}+\gamma V(S_{t+1})-V(S_t)}_{\delta_t \ (\text{TD error})}\big] $$
SARSA (on-policy)
$$ Q(S_t,A_t)\leftarrow Q(S_t,A_t)+\alpha\left[R_{t+1}+\gamma Q(S_{t+1},A_{t+1})-Q(S_t,A_t)\right] $$ \(A_{t+1}\) = the action actually taken next
Q-Learning (off-policy)
$$ Q(S_t,A_t)\leftarrow Q(S_t,A_t)+\alpha\left[R_{t+1}+\gamma\max_a Q(S_{t+1},a)-Q(S_t,A_t)\right] $$
Expected SARSA
$$ Q(S_t,A_t)\leftarrow Q(S_t,A_t)+\alpha\left[R_{t+1}+\gamma\sum_a \pi(a|S_{t+1})Q(S_{t+1},a)-Q(S_t,A_t)\right] $$
n-step Return
$$ G_{t:t+n}=R_{t+1}+\gamma R_{t+2}+\cdots+\gamma^{n-1}R_{t+n}+\gamma^n V(S_{t+n}) $$
Semi-Gradient TD(0) — Linear VFA
$$ \mathbf w \leftarrow \mathbf w + \alpha\,\delta_t\,\nabla \hat v(S_t,\mathbf w), \qquad \delta_t = R_{t+1}+\gamma\hat v(S_{t+1},\mathbf w)-\hat v(S_t,\mathbf w) $$ Linear case \(\hat v(s,\mathbf w)=\mathbf w^\top\mathbf x(s)\): gradient \(=\mathbf x(s)\) — only active tile weights update
DDQN Target
$$ y = r+\gamma\, Q\!\left(s',\ \underset{a'}{\arg\max}\,Q(s',a';\theta);\ \theta^-\right) $$ Online net \(\theta\) selects the action; target net \(\theta^-\) evaluates it
PPO Clipped Objective
$$ \mathcal L^{\text{CLIP}}(\theta)=\mathbb E_t\!\left[\min\!\big(r_t(\theta)A_t,\ \text{clip}(r_t(\theta),1{-}\varepsilon,1{+}\varepsilon)A_t\big)\right] $$ \(r_t(\theta)=\pi_\theta(A_t|S_t)/\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(A_t|S_t)\), typical \(\varepsilon=0.1\)–\(0.2\)
UCT (MCTS Selection)
$$ \text{UCT}(j)=\frac{X_j}{N_j}+c\sqrt{\frac{\ln N}{N_j}} $$ \(X_j/N_j\) = win rate (exploitation), second term = exploration bonus, \(N\) = parent visits
DAgger — Dataset Aggregation
$$ \mathcal D \leftarrow \mathcal D \cup \{(s,\pi^*(s)) : s \in \rho_{\pi_i}\} $$ Query the expert on states the agent's own policy visits, then retrain — error drops from \(O(T^2\epsilon)\) (BC) to \(O(T\epsilon)\) (DAgger)
Compounding Error Bound (Behaviour Cloning)
$$ J(\pi_{\text{BC}}) \ge J(\pi^*) - T^2\epsilon $$ \(T\) = horizon, \(\epsilon\) = per-step error — the quadratic blow-up is why BC fails on long tasks

Lesson 10 — Temporal Difference (TD) Methods High risk

TD(0) — one-step bootstrapping

TD(0) uses the observed reward plus its own estimate of the next state's value as the target: \( \text{target}=R_{t+1}+\gamma V(S_{t+1}) \). This is bootstrapping — updating an estimate using another estimate. Properties: online (updates every step, not every episode), model-free, biased but lower variance than MC, converges to \(v_\pi\) under standard step-size conditions.

On-policy (SARSA) vs Off-policy (Q-Learning)

  • SARSA: the policy used to select \(A_{t+1}\) and the policy being improved are the same. Safe — learns the value of the policy it actually follows, including exploratory steps.
  • Q-Learning: uses a behaviour policy (e.g. ε-greedy) to act, but the TD target is greedy (\(\max_a\)). It ignores which action was actually taken next.
  • Practical consequence (Cliff-Walking): SARSA learns the safe long path (avoids the cliff edge during exploration); Q-Learning learns the optimal short path but performs worse during learning because ε-greedy sometimes falls off.

Maximisation bias & Double Q-Learning

Q-Learning's \(\max_a Q(s',a)\) is almost always an overestimate when Q-values are noisy — a positive bias. Fix: maintain two independent tables \(Q_1,Q_2\). Action selection uses \(Q_1\): \(A^*=\arg\max_a Q_1(s',a)\); the target uses \(Q_2\): \(R+\gamma Q_2(s',A^*)\). Which table is updated is chosen randomly each step. Because the selector and evaluator are independent, the bias is eliminated.

Worked Example 1 — TD(0) step-by-step trace
Setup: states A, B, C. \(V(A)=V(B)=V(C)=0\). \(\alpha=0.5,\ \gamma=1\). Episode: \(A\xrightarrow{r=0}B\xrightarrow{r=0}C\xrightarrow{r=2}\)terminal.
Step 1 (A→B, r=0): \(\delta=0+1(0)-0=0\)  →  \(V(A)\leftarrow 0+0.5(0)=0\)
Step 2 (B→C, r=0): \(\delta=0\)  →  \(V(B)\leftarrow 0\)
Step 3 (C→terminal, r=2): \(\delta=2+1(0)-0=2\)  →  \(V(C)\leftarrow 0+0.5(2)=1.0\)
After 1 episode: \(V(C)=1.0\); \(V(A)=V(B)=0\) still — credit propagates back to A and B only over many episodes.
Worked Example 2 — SARSA vs Q-Learning on the same transition
\(Q(S,A)=5\). From \(S\), action \(A\) taken, arrive at \(S'\) with \(R=2\). At \(S'\): \(Q(S',a_1)=8,\ Q(S',a_2)=3\). ε-greedy selects \(a_2\) (exploratory). \(\alpha=0.1,\ \gamma=0.9\).
SARSA (uses actual next action \(a_2\)): \(Q\leftarrow 5+0.1[2+0.9(3)-5]=5+0.1(-0.3)=\mathbf{4.97}\)
Q-Learning (uses greedy next action \(a_1\)): \(Q\leftarrow 5+0.1[2+0.9(8)-5]=5+0.1(4.2)=\mathbf{5.42}\)
Key insight: SARSA reduces Q because it accounts for exploration (taking the worse action). Q-Learning increases Q because it assumes greedy action selection in the future.
Worked Example 3 — 2-step TD return
\(S_0\xrightarrow{R_1=1}S_1\xrightarrow{R_2=2}S_2\). \(V(S_2)=5,\ \gamma=0.9,\ \alpha=0.5,\ V(S_0)=0\).
\(G_{0:2}=R_1+\gamma R_2+\gamma^2 V(S_2)=1+0.9(2)+0.81(5)=1+1.8+4.05=6.85\)
\(V(S_0)\leftarrow 0+0.5(6.85-0)=3.425\) — compare with 1-step TD \(G_{0:1}=1+0.9V(S_1)\): the 2-step version uses more actual reward, reducing bias.
Exam tips — TD Methods The TD error \(\delta_t=R_{t+1}+\gamma V(S_{t+1})-V(S_t)\) appears in every update — memorise it. SARSA uses the taken next action; Q-Learning uses the max next action — that one word is the entire on/off-policy distinction. For Expected SARSA, compute the expectation using the policy probabilities, not just the max. Double Q-Learning exam question: "which table is updated?" → randomly one of the two; use the other to compute the target.

Lesson 11 — Race Car: DP vs MC vs TD Comparison

Method comparison on the same problem

AspectDPMC / TD
Environment modelRequired (transition probabilities)Not needed
Update timingFull sweeps of all statesAfter each step (TD) or episode (MC)
ConvergenceGuaranteed (finite MDP)TD: online, faster; MC: needs full episodes
Cliff handlingExplicitly models the crashLearns by experiencing crashes

SARSA on the Race Car: during training the car avoids the wall aggressively because it accounts for exploration noise — learns a conservative path. Q-Learning: learns the optimal (shorter, riskier) path because it always targets the greedy action in the update, but crashes more during ε-greedy exploration. Expected SARSA: sits between the two — averages over all possible next actions including exploratory ones — usually outperforms both at the same step size.

Worked Example — same transition, three methods
Car at position \(p\), takes action forward, reaches \(p'\), \(R=-1\). \(Q(p,\text{fwd})=-10,\ Q(p',\text{fwd})=-8,\ Q(p',\text{brake})=-12\). Policy: ε-greedy with ε=0.1, so \(\pi(\text{fwd}\mid p')=0.95,\ \pi(\text{brake}\mid p')=0.05\). Next action actually selected: brake (exploratory). \(\alpha=0.1,\gamma=0.9\).
SARSA (uses actual next action = brake): \(Q\leftarrow -10+0.1[-1+0.9(-12)-(-10)]=-10+0.1(-1.8)=\mathbf{-10.18}\)
Q-Learning (uses max action = fwd, \(-8\)): \(Q\leftarrow -10+0.1[-1+0.9(-8)-(-10)]=-10+0.1(1.8)=\mathbf{-9.82}\)
Expected SARSA: \(\mathbb E[Q(p',\cdot)]=0.95(-8)+0.05(-12)=-8.2\); \(Q\leftarrow -10+0.1[-1+0.9(-8.2)+10]=\mathbf{-9.838}\)
Interpretation: SARSA is most pessimistic (accounts for crash risk from exploration). Q-Learning is most optimistic. Expected SARSA sits in between.
Exam tips — Race Car Typical question types: (1) trace 2–3 TD/SARSA/Q-Learning updates numerically; (2) explain why SARSA learns a safer path; (3) compare convergence guarantees. Off-policy (Q-Learning) converges to the optimal policy regardless of behaviour policy — as long as all state-action pairs are visited sufficiently. MC needs the entire episode to compute \(G_t\) — if episodes are very long, TD is far more practical.

Lesson 12 — Value Function Approximation & DQN High risk

Why linear VFA / tile coding

When the state space is continuous we cannot store one value per state — VFA learns a compact parameterised representation. Coarse coding: overlapping circles (receptive fields) cover the state space; a state activates every feature whose circle contains it. Tile coding: partition the state space into a grid (one "tiling"), then shift and overlay \(k\) such grids ("tilings"). Each tiling contributes one active feature per state; \(\mathbf x(s)\) is binary with \(k\) ones. Linear VFA: \(\hat v(s,\mathbf w)=\mathbf w^\top\mathbf x(s)\), so \(\nabla \hat v(s,\mathbf w)=\mathbf x(s)\) — the update \(w_i \leftarrow w_i+\alpha\delta_t x_i(s)\) touches only active features.

DQN — three key tricks

  1. Deep neural network as function approximator: takes raw state as input, outputs \(Q(s,a)\) for all actions simultaneously.
  2. Experience replay: store transitions \((s,a,r,s')\) in a replay buffer \(\mathcal D\); sample random mini-batches for each gradient update. This breaks temporal correlations that destabilise training and allows data reuse.
  3. Target network \(\theta^-\): a second copy of the network whose weights are frozen for \(C\) steps, then synchronised. This keeps the TD target stable — avoids a moving target during optimisation.

DDQN decouples action selection (online net \(\theta\)) from action evaluation (target net \(\theta^-\)). Eliminates the maximisation bias that inflates Q-values in vanilla DQN.

Worked Example — linear VFA with tile coding
\(\mathbf x(s_t)=[1,0,1,0]^\top,\ \mathbf x(s_{t+1})=[0,1,1,0]^\top\). \(\mathbf w=[2,3,1,5]^\top,\ \alpha=0.1,\gamma=0.9,\ R_{t+1}=4\).
\(\hat v(s_t,\mathbf w)=\mathbf w^\top\mathbf x(s_t)=2(1)+3(0)+1(1)+5(0)=3\)
\(\hat v(s_{t+1},\mathbf w)=2(0)+3(1)+1(1)+5(0)=4\)
\(\delta_t=4+0.9(4)-3=4.6\)
\(\mathbf w\leftarrow \mathbf w+0.1(4.6)[1,0,1,0]^\top=[\mathbf{2.46},\,3,\,\mathbf{1.46},\,5]\)
Only \(w_1\) and \(w_3\) change (active tile features for \(s_t\)); \(w_2, w_4\) unchanged.
Worked Example — DQN vs DDQN target
Transition \((s,a,r{=}1,s')\). Online net: \(Q(s',a_1;\theta)=9,\ Q(s',a_2;\theta)=6\). Target net: \(Q(s',a_1;\theta^-)=7,\ Q(s',a_2;\theta^-)=8,\ \gamma=0.9\).
DQN target (both select and evaluate with \(\theta^-\)): \(y=1+0.9\max(7,8)=1+7.2=\mathbf{8.2}\)
DDQN target: online net selects \(a^*=\arg\max Q(s',\cdot;\theta)=a_1\) (since 9>6); target evaluates it: \(y=1+0.9(7)=\mathbf{7.3}\)
When online and target disagree on ranking, DDQN gives the lower (less inflated) target — the bias correction in action.
Exam tips — VFA & DQN "Semi-gradient" = stop the gradient through the target — the target \(r+\gamma\hat v(s',\mathbf w)\) is treated as a fixed number when differentiating w.r.t. \(\mathbf w\). "Purpose of the target network?" → stable TD target, prevents divergence. "Purpose of experience replay?" → breaks temporal correlation, enables mini-batch updates, allows data reuse. DDQN: online net selects the action, target net evaluates it — don't confuse the two roles.

Lesson 13 — Policy Gradient Methods High risk

Why Policy Gradient?

Value-based methods (Q-Learning, SARSA) learn a value function and derive a policy. Policy gradient methods directly parameterise the policy \(\pi_\theta(a|s)\) and optimise \(J(\theta)\) by gradient ascent. Advantages: works naturally in continuous action spaces, can represent stochastic policies directly, better convergence properties in some settings. Score function trick: \(\nabla\pi(a|s,\theta)=\pi(a|s,\theta)\cdot\nabla\ln\pi(a|s,\theta)\) — lets us write the gradient as an expectation estimable from samples.

REINFORCE (Monte-Carlo policy gradient)

For each complete episode: run under \(\pi_\theta\); for each step compute return \(G_t\); update \(\theta\leftarrow\theta+\alpha G_t\nabla\ln\pi(A_t|S_t,\theta)\). Intuition: if \(G_t\) is large (good trajectory) we increase the probability of \(A_t\) in \(S_t\); if small, decrease it. Problem: high variance because \(G_t\) depends on the entire future trajectory. Fix: subtract a baseline (usually \(V(S_t)\)) to get the advantage \(A_t=G_t-V(S_t)\) — subtracting a state-dependent baseline is unbiased because \(\mathbb E[b(s)\nabla\ln\pi]=0\), but it reduces variance.

Actor-Critic & PPO

Actor: the policy \(\pi_\theta(a|s)\) — decides what action to take. Critic: value function \(V_w(s)\) — evaluates how good the current state is. A2C update uses advantage \(A_t=R_{t+1}+\gamma V_w(S_{t+1})-V_w(S_t)\) (TD-based, online, lower variance than a full MC return). PPO limits how much the policy changes per update by clipping the probability ratio: if \(A_t>0\) (good action) allow \(r_t\) up to \(1+\varepsilon\) but no further; if \(A_t<0\) (bad action) allow \(r_t\) down to \(1-\varepsilon\) but no lower. This prevents catastrophically large policy updates. DPO (Direct Preference Optimisation) skips the reward model entirely — fine-tunes directly from preference pairs, treating the base policy as an implicit reward function.

Worked Example — REINFORCE on a 2-action softmax policy
\(\pi_\theta(a|s)=\text{softmax}(\theta_a)\) for \(a\in\{L,R\}\). \(\theta_L=1.0,\ \theta_R=2.0\).
\(\pi(L|s)=e^{1.0}/(e^{1.0}+e^{2.0})=2.718/10.107\approx0.269,\quad \pi(R|s)\approx0.731\)
Episode result: action \(R\) taken, return \(G_0=3\). \(\alpha=0.01\).
Gradient of log-prob (softmax): \(\nabla_{\theta_a}\ln\pi(a'|s)=\mathbf 1[a'=a]-\pi(a|s)\)
\(\nabla_{\theta_R}\ln\pi(R|s)=1-0.731=0.269;\quad \nabla_{\theta_L}\ln\pi(R|s)=0-0.731=-0.731\)
\(\theta_R\leftarrow 2.0+0.01(3)(0.269)=\mathbf{2.008};\quad \theta_L\leftarrow 1.0+0.01(3)(-0.731)=\mathbf{0.978}\)
Result: \(\pi(R|s)\) increases, \(\pi(L|s)\) decreases — the good action \(R\) becomes more likely.

Lesson 14 — Model-Based RL: MCTS and AlphaGo/Zero

Model-based vs model-free

A model is a learned or given function \(P(s'|s,a),R(s,a)\). With a model, the agent can plan (simulate future trajectories without actually acting). Advantages: far more sample efficient — can learn from imagined experience; ideal for costly environments (robotics, medical). Disadvantages: model errors compound; planning with a wrong model leads to suboptimal policies. Spectrum: pure model-free (Q-Learning, DQN) → hybrid (AlphaZero: MCTS for planning + neural nets for policy/value) → pure planning (MCTS with a perfect model).

MCTS — 4 phases (repeat until compute budget exhausted)

  1. Selection: start at root; use UCT to pick a child until reaching a leaf or unvisited node.
  2. Expansion: add one or more child nodes to the leaf.
  3. Simulation (rollout): play random (or policy-guided) moves to the end of the game.
  4. Backpropagation: update visit counts and values along the path back to the root.

AlphaGo → AlphaZero → MuZero → PlaNet

SystemKey innovation
AlphaGoSupervised learning on human game records to initialise policy/value nets + MCTS rollouts. First to beat a professional Go player.
AlphaZeroNo human data. Pure self-play from scratch. Single neural network for policy + value. MCTS provides improved training targets.
MuZeroLearns a latent model of the environment (no true simulator needed). Plans in latent space. Generalises to Atari and board games.

Lesson 15 — Imitation Learning

Behavioural Cloning & distributional shift

BC treats imitation as supervised learning: input = state \(s\), label = expert action \(a\). Train \(\pi_\theta\) to minimise prediction error on expert data \(\mathcal D\). Distributional shift problem: the policy is trained on the expert's state distribution \(\rho_{\pi^*}\). At test time even small mistakes push the agent into states not seen in training — from those unseen states the policy makes more mistakes, compounding further. This is covariate shift. Compounding error: with horizon \(T\) and per-step error \(\epsilon\), total error grows as \(O(T^2\epsilon)\) — quadratic in horizon, catastrophic for long-horizon tasks.

DAgger — Dataset Aggregation (Ross & Bagnell, 2011)

DAgger fixes the distributional shift by ensuring the agent trains on its own state distribution. Algorithm: (1) train \(\pi_1\) from expert demos \(\mathcal D_0\); (2) for \(i=1,2,\ldots\): run \(\pi_i\) in the environment, collect visited states \(\mathcal S_i\); query the expert for correct actions \(\pi^*(s)\) on each \(s\in\mathcal S_i\); aggregate \(\mathcal D\leftarrow\mathcal D\cup\{(s,\pi^*(s))\}\); retrain \(\pi_{i+1}\) on all data so far. Over iterations, \(\mathcal D\) covers the agent's own state distribution — error bound improves to \(O(T\epsilon)\), linear instead of quadratic. Limitation: requires the expert to be queryable at any arbitrary state — costly or infeasible for a human expert.

Inverse RL and GAIL

Inverse RL (IRL): instead of copying actions, IRL learns why the expert acts as it does by recovering the reward function \(R^*(s,a)\); standard RL is then applied to find the optimal policy under \(R^*\). Captures the expert's intent; handles goal changes gracefully, but is ill-posed (many reward functions can explain the same behaviour) and expensive (a nested RL loop per reward hypothesis). GAIL (Generative Adversarial Imitation Learning) frames imitation as a GAN problem: a discriminator \(D_\psi(s,a)\) is trained to distinguish agent trajectories from expert trajectories; the generator (the policy \(\pi_\theta\)) is trained via RL to fool the discriminator. GAIL bypasses explicit reward engineering — the discriminator is the reward signal — and matches expert performance with far fewer demonstrations than BC.

Worked Example — BC vs DAgger on a 5-state chain
Chain: \(s_1\to s_2\to s_3\to s_4\to s_5\) (goal). Expert always moves right.
BC training: expert demos cover \(s_1\to s_2\to s_3\to s_4\to s_5\). After training, \(\pi_{\text{BC}}\) makes an error at \(s_3\) (goes left to \(s_2\)). Now at \(s_2\), the policy has never seen "recovery after visiting \(s_3\)" — the error compounds.
DAgger iteration 1: run \(\pi_1\) (BC policy). Agent reaches \(s_3\), goes left to \(s_2\), then \(s_1\). Query expert at \(s_2\) (after backtrack) and \(s_1\): expert says "go right". Add \((s_2,\text{right}),(s_1,\text{right})\) from the agent's own trajectory to \(\mathcal D\).
DAgger iteration 2: train on the aggregated \(\mathcal D\). Now the policy knows what to do at \(s_2\) even when arriving there via backtrack. Error reduces because it has seen its own error states.
Key difference: BC trains only on \(\rho_{\pi^*}\). DAgger trains on \(\rho_{\pi_i}\) (the agent's own failures) labelled by the expert.
Exam tips — Imitation Learning DAgger vs BC: DAgger queries the expert on agent-visited states; BC only uses offline expert data. Compounding error grows as \(O(T^2)\) for BC, \(O(T)\) for DAgger. GAIL's discriminator is the surrogate reward; the generator is the policy trained with RL. IRL learns the reward function, not a policy. In AlphaGo/AlphaZero, the initial supervised phase on human data is itself a form of Behavioural Cloning.

Full 6-Mark Model Answers — Lessons 10–15 Theory Questions

Model Answer (6 marks): Explain TD Learning and compare with DP and MC

(1) Temporal Difference Learning (2 marks): TD learning updates value estimates online after each step using bootstrapping: the target \(R_{t+1}+\gamma V(S_{t+1})\) blends one step of actual reward with the current estimate. It is model-free (no \(P(s'|s,a)\) needed) and does not require complete episodes.

(2) vs Dynamic Programming (2 marks): DP requires a full model of the environment to perform Bellman sweeps over all states. It is model-based and offline. TD requires no model and updates incrementally from experience. Both bootstrap (use current value estimates), but DP updates all states while TD updates only visited states.

(3) vs Monte Carlo (2 marks): MC waits for the end of an episode to compute the actual return \(G_t\). This gives unbiased estimates but is impractical for long or continuing tasks. TD bootstraps with a one-step target — biased but much lower variance. TD can also learn in continuing (non-episodic) tasks where MC cannot.

Model Answer (6 marks): Explain DQN and its key innovations

(1) DQN overview (1 mark): DQN (Deep Q-Network, Mnih et al. 2015) extends Q-Learning to use a deep neural network to approximate \(Q(s,a;\theta)\). It achieved human-level performance on 49 Atari games from raw pixels.

(2) Experience Replay (2 marks): Transitions \((s,a,r,s')\) are stored in a replay buffer \(\mathcal D\). Mini-batches are sampled uniformly at random for gradient updates. This (a) breaks temporal correlations between consecutive transitions, which destabilise training; (b) allows data reuse; (c) smooths the data distribution over many past behaviours.

(3) Target Network (2 marks): A second network \(\theta^-\) provides the TD target \(y=r+\gamma\max_{a'}Q(s',a';\theta^-)\). Its weights are frozen for \(C\) steps then copied from \(\theta\). This stabilises training by preventing the "moving target" problem where the target changes as fast as the estimate.

(4) DDQN improvement (1 mark): Vanilla DQN suffers from maximisation bias because it uses the same network to select and evaluate the best next action. DDQN uses \(\theta\) to select \(a^*=\arg\max_{a'}Q(s',a';\theta)\) and \(\theta^-\) to evaluate it: \(y=r+\gamma Q(s',a^*;\theta^-)\), reducing overestimation.

Model Answer (6 marks): Explain the Policy Gradient Theorem and REINFORCE

(1) Policy Gradient Theorem (2 marks): For a parameterised policy \(\pi_\theta\), the gradient of the performance objective \(J(\theta)=v_{\pi_\theta}(s_0)\) is \(\nabla_\theta J(\theta)\propto \mathbb E_\pi[G_t\cdot\nabla_\theta\ln\pi(A_t|S_t,\theta)]\). This is remarkable: the gradient of expected return can be estimated from samples without knowing the environment dynamics. The score function \(\nabla\ln\pi\) points in the direction of increasing action probability.

(2) REINFORCE algorithm (2 marks): REINFORCE is a Monte Carlo instantiation: collect a full episode, compute returns \(G_t\) at each step, and update \(\theta\leftarrow\theta+\alpha G_t\nabla\ln\pi(A_t|S_t,\theta)\). High-return episodes increase the probability of the actions taken; low-return episodes decrease them. The algorithm is unbiased but has high variance.

(3) Baseline and variance reduction (2 marks): Subtracting a baseline \(b(S_t)\) from \(G_t\) reduces variance without introducing bias: \(\mathbb E[b(s)\nabla\ln\pi]=0\) for any state-dependent baseline. Using \(b(S_t)=V(S_t)\) gives the advantage \(A_t=G_t-V(S_t)\). A2C further reduces variance by using a TD-based critic instead of the full MC return, combining the benefits of policy gradient and value function learning.

Model Answer (6 marks): Explain MCTS and how it is used in AlphaZero

(1) MCTS overview (1 mark): Monte Carlo Tree Search is a planning algorithm that builds a search tree by interleaving simulation (random or policy-guided rollouts) with back-propagation of results. It does not require a heuristic evaluation function.

(2) Four phases (2 marks): (a) Selection: traverse the tree from the root, always picking the child with the highest UCT score, until reaching a leaf or unexpanded node. (b) Expansion: add one or more children to the leaf. (c) Simulation: run a rollout from the new node to a terminal state. (d) Backpropagation: update visit counts \(N\) and value estimates \(W\) for all nodes on the path.

(3) UCT formula (1 mark): \(\text{UCT}(j)=X_j/N_j+c\sqrt{\ln N/N_j}\). The first term is exploitation (win rate); the second is exploration (favours less-visited nodes). Parameter \(c\) balances the two.

(4) AlphaZero integration (2 marks): AlphaZero replaces random rollouts with a neural network \(f_\theta(s)=(\mathbf p,v)\) that outputs a policy vector \(\mathbf p\) (prior for move selection) and a value \(v\) (position evaluation). MCTS uses \(\mathbf p\) to bias UCT selection and \(v\) as the rollout result instead of full random play. The network is trained on \((s,\boldsymbol\pi,z)\) tuples from self-play, where \(\boldsymbol\pi\) are the MCTS visit counts (a better policy than the raw network) and \(z\) is the game outcome — a self-improving loop.

$$ \mathcal{L}_{\text{BC}}(\theta) = -\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \log \pi_\theta(a_i^E \mid s_i) $$

where \((s_i, a_i^E)\) are expert state-action pairs.

Failure mode: covariate shift. At test time, the learned policy makes small errors that lead to states not seen in the expert dataset. The policy has no training signal for these novel states → errors compound → catastrophic failure far from the expert distribution.

(d-2) How does DAGGER modify data collection vs BC? Why does this help with the policy's own mistakes? [1.5 Marks]
Show Answer — Part (d-2)

BC: Collects expert demos once on expert's state distribution. Policy trained offline on this fixed dataset.

DAGGER: Iteratively (1) rolls out the learned policy to collect trajectories, (2) queries the expert for the correct action at each visited state, (3) adds these to the dataset, (4) retrains.

Why it helps: DAGGER trains the policy on the states it actually visits, not just expert-visited states. When the policy makes a mistake and reaches a novel state, it now has expert-labelled training data for that state → learns to recover → distribution shift is eliminated over iterations.
