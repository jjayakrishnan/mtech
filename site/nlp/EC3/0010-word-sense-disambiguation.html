1 · Word Senses

Analogy

"I need to go to the bank." — The bank near the river? The financial bank? This single word has multiple senses — distinct meanings. WSD is the task of picking the right one from context.

Key Terms
  • Lexeme: A form–meaning pair (one entry in a dictionary).
  • Lemma / citation form: Grammatical base form. "carpets" → lemma "carpet".
  • Sense: A discrete representation of one aspect of a word's meaning. The lemma "bank" has two senses: financial institution and riverside.

2 · Relations Between Senses

RelationDefinitionExample
HomonymySame form, unrelated meaningsbat (mammal) vs bat (cricket bat)
PolysemySame form, related (extended) meaningsbank (building) vs bank (institution)
SynonymyDifferent forms, same (or very similar) meaningcouch / sofa; automobile / car
AntonymyOpposites along one semantic dimensionhot / cold; rise / fall
HyponymyOne sense is a subclass of anothercar is a hyponym of vehicle
HypernymySuperordinate/generalizationvehicle is a hypernym of car
Exam Trap

Synonymy is a relation between senses, not words. "big" and "large" are synonymous in "a big/large car" but not in "a big sister" (*"a large sister"). The specific sense matters.

3 · WordNet

What Is WordNet?

WordNet is a large hierarchically organized lexical database for English. It groups words into synsets (synonym sets) — each synset represents a distinct concept.

CategoryEntries
Nouns117,097
Verbs11,488
Adjectives22,141
Adverbs4,601

Average noun has 1.23 senses; average verb has 2.16 senses.

WordNet Synsets & Relations

  • Synset: {car, auto, automobile, machine, motorcar} — all share the same concept
  • Hypernym/Hyponym: vehicle → car → sedan (IS-A hierarchy)
  • Meronym: wheel is a part of car (HAS-PART)
  • Holonym: car is part of traffic (PART-OF)
  • Gloss: Dictionary-style definition stored with each synset

Semantic Similarity via WordNet

The WordNet hierarchy allows computing semantic similarity. Two senses are similar if their Lowest Common Subsumer (LCS) — the deepest shared ancestor — is specific:

$$\text{sim}_{path}(c_1, c_2) = \frac{1}{\text{PathLen}(c_1, c_2) + 1}$$
$$\text{sim}_{WUP}(c_1, c_2) = \frac{2 \cdot \text{depth}(\text{LCS})}{\text{depth}(c_1) + \text{depth}(c_2)}$$

4 · Word Sense Disambiguation (WSD)

Problem Definition

Given a word \(w\) in context \(C\), select the correct sense \(s^*\) from the inventory \(\{s_1, s_2, \ldots, s_k\}\):

$$s^* = \arg\max_{s_i} P(s_i \mid w, C)$$

Why WSD is Hard

  • Ambiguity is ubiquitous: Most common words have 3–10 senses in WordNet
  • Context dependency: "I saw the bank" — financial or riverside depends on unseen context
  • Granularity: Are "bank (building)" and "bank (institution)" one sense or two?
  • Lack of labelled data: Sense-tagged corpora are expensive to create (knowledge acquisition bottleneck)

5 · Lesk Algorithm

Analogy

If the dictionary definition of "bank" (sense 1) shares many words with the surrounding text, pick that sense. It's like comparing the context to each definition and choosing the best match.

Simplified Lesk Algorithm
for each sense s_i of word w: overlap_i = |gloss(s_i) ∩ context(w)| return argmax_i overlap_i

Where gloss(s_i) is the WordNet definition + example sentences of sense \(s_i\), and context(w) is the surrounding words in the text.

Worked Example

Disambiguating "bank" in "I deposited money at the bank"
  • Sense 1 (financial): gloss = "a financial institution that accepts deposits, makes loans, and handles monetary transactions" → overlap with {deposited, money} = 2 ✓
  • Sense 2 (riverside): gloss = "sloping land beside a river or lake" → overlap = 0
  • → Select Sense 1 ✓

Lesk Variants

VariantImprovement
Simplified LeskOnly uses gloss of target word's sense
Extended LeskAlso includes glosses of related synsets (hypernyms, hyponyms, siblings)
Adapted LeskWeights overlap by IDF (rare words count more)
Embedding LeskReplaces word overlap with cosine similarity of averaged embeddings

6 · Supervised WSD

Train a classifier per word using sense-tagged corpora (e.g., SemCor).

Features

  • Surrounding words (bag-of-words window ±3)
  • POS tags of surrounding words
  • Local collocations (bigrams/trigrams)
  • Syntactic features (dependency head, subject/object role)

Most Frequent Sense (MFS) Baseline

Important Baseline

Always predict the most frequent sense in training data. This is surprisingly strong: ~65% accuracy on standard benchmarks. Any WSD system must beat this to be useful. Systems that don't beat MFS have failed.

Knowledge Acquisition Bottleneck

The core challenge: creating large sense-tagged corpora is extremely expensive. Solutions:

  • Bootstrap: Use MFS seed data + self-training
  • Transfer: Use parallel corpora — sense in language A disambiguates sense in language B
  • Pre-trained models: BERT produces contextual embeddings — sense naturally encoded

7 · BERT-Based WSD

BERT produces contextual embeddings — the same word gets different vectors in different contexts. This naturally encodes sense distinctions without explicit sense labelling.

Two Approaches

Approach 1 — 1-Nearest Neighbour (BERT-Embed)
  1. For each sense \(s_i\): collect all training sentences where \(w\) has sense \(s_i\)
  2. Average BERT embedding over those occurrences → sense prototype \(\mathbf{v}_{s_i}\)
  3. At test time: get BERT embedding \(\mathbf{v}_{test}\) of the target word
  4. Return \(s^* = \arg\max_{s_i} \cos(\mathbf{v}_{test}, \mathbf{v}_{s_i})\)
Approach 2 — Fine-tuned Classifier (GlossBERT)
  • Concatenate [CLS] context [SEP] gloss [SEP] and fine-tune on SemCor
  • Score = sigmoid of [CLS] representation → binary: "is this the right sense?"
  • Run for all senses, pick highest score

9 · Practice Questions

Q1 · Distinguish homonymy from polysemy with examples.

Homonymy: Same spelling/sound, completely unrelated meanings. Example: bat (cricket implement) vs bat (flying mammal). Different etymologies.
Polysemy: Same word, multiple related meanings derived from a common origin. Example: bank (financial institution) vs bank (building where banking happens) — related because one implies the other. The distinction: polysemy has related senses; homonymy doesn't.

Q2 · Explain the Simplified Lesk algorithm step by step.

1. Extract the context: words in the surrounding window (e.g., ±5 words) around the target word.
2. For each sense \(s_i\): get its gloss (definition + examples) from WordNet.
3. Compute overlap = number of words in common between gloss and context.
4. Select the sense with maximum overlap.
Weakness: Glosses are short, so overlap is often 0 or 1; Extended Lesk adds related synset glosses to improve recall.

Q3 · What is the Most Frequent Sense baseline and why does it matter?

MFS always selects the most frequent sense of a word as listed in training data (SemCor). It achieves ~65% accuracy on standard benchmarks without any context-specific analysis. It matters because: (a) it's easy to compute; (b) it's surprisingly strong; (c) any WSD system that doesn't exceed MFS is not useful — it proves the model isn't leveraging context at all.

Q4 (6-mark) · Compare knowledge-based (Lesk) and supervised (BERT) WSD approaches.

Knowledge-based (Lesk): Uses WordNet glosses; no labelled training data needed; works for all words with entries in WordNet; limited by gloss length and quality; Extended Lesk ~50–60% F1.
Supervised (SVM/features): Trains word-specific classifiers; requires sense-tagged corpus (SemCor); strong accuracy ~70% F1; but suffers from knowledge acquisition bottleneck — rare words have too few examples.
BERT-based: Produces context-sensitive embeddings; 1-NN approach needs few examples per sense; GlossBERT adds gloss as additional signal; SOTA ~78% F1; generalises better across domains than feature-based.
Trade-off: Knowledge-based is language-agnostic and requires no data; supervised/neural needs annotation but achieves higher accuracy.
