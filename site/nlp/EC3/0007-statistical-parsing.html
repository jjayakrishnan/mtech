1 · Why Parsing Matters

Analogy

"I saw a girl with a telescope." — Does the telescope belong to you or the girl? Parsing resolves this: the same string has two different tree structures with entirely different meanings.

Parsing is the problem of taking a string of words and determining its grammatical structure — building a parse tree. Without parsing, NLP systems can't distinguish sentiment, resolve references, or translate phrases.

Applications

  • Sentiment Analysis: "I do not like Frozen" ≠ "I like frozen yogurt" — structure matters.
  • Relation Extraction: "Rome is the capital of Italy" — which is capital of which?
  • Machine Translation: Choose between translation candidates by parse structure.
  • Speech Recognition: Parsing scores candidate transcripts.
  • Question Answering: Parse the question to extract the answer pattern.
Exam Trap

Ambiguity in natural language is exponential — not just a few alternatives. A sentence with 20 words can have millions of valid parse trees. This is why naive brute-force parsing doesn't work.

2 · Context-Free Grammars (CFG)

Definition

A CFG is a 4-tuple \( G = (N, \Sigma, R, S) \):

  • \(N\) — set of non-terminals (e.g., NP, VP, S)
  • \(\Sigma\) — set of terminals (the vocabulary)
  • \(R\) — set of rules: each is \(A \to \beta\) where \(A \in N\) and \(\beta \in (N \cup \Sigma)^*\)
  • \(S\) — the start symbol

A CFG rule has exactly one non-terminal on the left — "context-free" means the expansion doesn't depend on surrounding context.

Phrase Categories — what they mean with real examples

Every phrase type is named after its head word (the most important word inside it). The head determines the phrase type — everything else modifies it.

PhraseShortHeadPlain EnglishExamples from real sentences
Noun PhraseNP Noun A "thing" chunk — answers who or what NLP   the cat   very interesting systems
Verb PhraseVP Verb An "action" chunk — what the NP does or is is interesting   ate the cake   are NLP systems
Adjective PhraseADJP Adjective A "description" chunk — how something is very interesting   quite fast   really good
Adverb PhraseADVP Adverb A "manner/degree" chunk — modifies a verb or adjective very quickly   quite slowly   extremely well
Prepositional PhrasePP Preposition A "location/relation" chunk — starts with in/on/with/for/of in the lab   on the table   with a fork
Quick trick to identify any phrase

Ask: "What kind of word is in charge here?"

  • If a noun is in charge → NP  ("the cat", "NLP")
  • If a verb is in charge → VP  ("is very interesting")
  • If an adjective is in charge → ADJP  "very interesting"
  • If a preposition is in charge → PP  "in the lab"

How to read this tree
  • Each leaf (bottom) = a real word from the sentence.
  • Each internal node = a grammar rule that fired. The label (NP, VP, S…) is the left-hand side of the rule.

  • The tree root = S (the whole sentence). If you reach S, the sentence is grammatical.

3 · Chart Parsing

Analogy

Chart parsing is dynamic programming for grammars. Think of it like a spreadsheet: once you know "words 2–4 form an NP", you record it once and reuse it without recomputing. The "chart" is that spreadsheet.

A chart stores edges — each edge records a partial or complete rule match over a span \([i, j]\) of the input.

Key Terms
  • Active arc: A partially matched rule. E.g., NP → ART • ADJ N means ART has been matched, waiting for ADJ N.
  • Passive arc: A completely matched constituent over span \([i,j]\).
  • Dot notation: The dot • marks how far we've matched. NP → ART ADJ • N means we need one more N.

Chart Parsing Advantages

  1. No repeated computation of the same sub-problem (unlike backtracking search)
  2. Handles left-recursive grammars without infinite loops
  3. Handles ambiguity gracefully — multiple parses stored simultaneously
  4. Earley algorithm runs in \(O(n^3)\) for general CFGs
Time complexity: \(\quad O(n^3)\) for Earley / CKY \(\quad\) where \(n\) = sentence length

4 · Probabilistic Context-Free Grammars (PCFG)

CFGs tell us what's possible; PCFGs tell us which parse is most likely. Each rule gets a probability.

Formal Definition

A PCFG = CFG + a probability for every rule such that for every non-terminal \(A\):

$$\sum_{j} P(A \to \xi_j) = 1$$

The probability of a parse tree \(T\) is the product of all rule probabilities used:

$$P(T) = \prod_{i=1}^{k} P(r_i)$$

The probability of a sentence is the sum over all its parse trees:

$$P(w_1^m) = \sum_{t: \text{yield}(t)=w_1^m} P(t)$$

Learning PCFG Probabilities

From a treebank (annotated corpus of parsed sentences), estimate by counting:

$$P(A \to \beta) = \frac{\text{Count}(A \to \beta)}{\text{Count}(A)}$$

The Penn WSJ Treebank (~50,000 sentences) is the standard training set.

Lexical Probability Example

Worked Example

Suppose we see "fish" appearing as:

  • Noun (N) 70 times, Verb (V) 30 times out of 100 total occurrences
  • \(P(\text{N} \to \text{fish}) = 0.70\), \(P(\text{V} \to \text{fish}) = 0.30\)

The parser will prefer the NP reading in ambiguous sentences.

Properties of PCFGs

  • Structural probabilities: P(A→β) captures which expansions are common, not lexical co-occurrence.
  • Good for: Grammar induction, robust parsing, probabilistic language models.
  • Weakness: Independence assumption — probabilities don't depend on parent or sibling nodes.
  • Fix: Lexicalized PCFGs (Collins, Charniak) add head word to each rule.
Exam Trap

PCFG probabilities are conditional: \(P(A \to \beta) = P(A \to \beta \mid A)\). Each non-terminal's rules must sum to 1. Don't confuse rule probability with joint probability of the whole tree.

5 · CKY Algorithm

Everyday picture — building with Lego bricks

Think of the sentence as a row of Lego bricks. You can only join two bricks if the grammar has a rule for that combination. CKY tries every possible way to split a span into two halves, checks if any rule matches, and records the best result. Small spans first — then use those to build bigger spans.

What "cell [i, j]" means

Cell [i, j] answers the question: "What grammar symbol(s) can cover words i through j?"

For a 3-word sentence "NLP is interesting" (words at positions 0, 1, 2):

  • [0,1] = "NLP" alone
  • [1,2] = "is" alone
  • [2,3] = "interesting" alone
  • [0,2] = "NLP is"
  • [1,3] = "is interesting"
  • [0,3] = "NLP is interesting" ← the full sentence. If S is here, we have a parse!

CKY requires the grammar to be in Chomsky Normal Form (CNF): every rule is either \(A \to BC\) (exactly two symbols) or \(A \to w\) (one terminal word). Unit rules like NP→N are eliminated by substitution first.

CKY Algorithm — how to read the pseudocode

Step 1 — Fill diagonal (length-1 spans, one word each): for j = 1 to n: table[j-1][j] = lexical rules for word[j] -- e.g., "NLP" → {NP:1.0} "is" → {V:1.0} "interesting" → {AdjP:1.0} Step 2 — Fill upper triangle (longer spans, bottom-up): for length = 2 to n: -- try span sizes 2, 3, ... n for start = 0 to n-length: -- slide the span across the sentence end = start + length for mid = start+1 to end-1: -- try every split point inside the span for each rule A → B C: p = table[start][mid][B] -- prob that B covers left half * table[mid][end][C] -- prob that C covers right half * P(A→BC) -- rule probability if p > table[start][end][A]: table[start][end][A] = p -- keep best probability
Key insight

Every cell [start, end] is computed by trying every split point mid between start and end. At each split you ask: "Is there a rule A→B C where B covers [start,mid] and C covers [mid,end]?" If yes, record A in [start,end].

Exam procedure — fill CKY in 3 phases
  1. Phase 1 — Diagonal: Write word→terminal in each [i, i+1] cell.
  2. Phase 2 — Length-2 spans: For each adjacent pair [i,i+1] and [i+1,i+2], try all rules. Write any that fire in [i, i+2].
  3. Phase 3 — Longer spans: For span [i,j], try every mid between i and j. At each mid, check left=[i,mid] and right=[mid,j]. If any rule A→B C matches, write A in [i,j] with probability = P(left) × P(right) × P(rule).
  4. Done when you reach [0,n]. If S is there, the sentence is grammatical.

CKY Algorithm (Probabilistic) — the pseudocode, now readable

for j = 1 to n: -- Phase 1: one word at a time table[j-1][j] = lexical probs for word[j] for length = 2 to n: -- Phase 2+3: spans of size 2, 3, ..., n for start = 0 to n-length: -- slide span across sentence end = start + length for mid = start+1 to end-1: -- try every split point for each rule A → B C: p = table[start][mid][B] -- left half prob * table[mid][end][C] -- right half prob * P(A→BC) -- rule prob if p > table[start][end][A]: table[start][end][A] = p -- keep best

6 · Parser Evaluation

MetricFormulaMeasures
Labelled Precision (LP)\(\frac{\text{correct brackets}}{\text{brackets in output}}\)Quality of parser output
Labelled Recall (LR)\(\frac{\text{correct brackets}}{\text{brackets in gold}}\)Coverage of gold standard
F1\(\frac{2 \cdot LP \cdot LR}{LP + LR}\)Harmonic mean
Exam Note

A "bracket" is a (label, start, end) triple — e.g., (NP, 0, 2). Both the label and span must match for it to count as correct in labelled evaluation. For unlabelled, only span positions matter.

Problems with Pure PCFGs

  • Structural probs only: P(VP→V NP) same regardless of head verb.
  • Solution — Lexicalized PCFGs: rule probability includes head word, e.g., \(P(\text{VP}(eat) \to V\,NP)\).
  • Attachment ambiguity: "I ate fish with a fork" — PP attaches to VP or NP? Lexical co-occurrence resolves this better than structure alone.

8 · Practice Questions

Q1 · What is the time complexity of CKY and why?

\(O(n^3 \cdot |G|)\) where \(n\) is sentence length and \(|G|\) is grammar size. Three nested loops: span length, start position, split point. Each cell lookup is O(1). Grammar size is constant at test time, so effectively \(O(n^3)\).

Q2 · How do PCFGs resolve structural ambiguity?

By assigning probabilities to each rule, PCFGs rank competing parse trees. The most probable tree (arg max over all parse trees) is selected. Probabilities are estimated from treebank frequencies: \(P(A \to \beta) = \text{Count}(A \to \beta) / \text{Count}(A)\).

Q3 · What is Chomsky Normal Form and why does CKY need it?

CNF allows only rules of the form \(A \to BC\) or \(A \to w\). CKY needs CNF because it tries all possible binary splits of a span at a midpoint. With 3-ary rules, the split structure becomes undefined. Any CFG can be converted to CNF without changing the language generated.

Q4 (6-mark) · Compare top-down, bottom-up, and chart parsing. Which is best and why?

Top-down: Starts from S, predicts what to look for in the input. Risk: tries impossible expansions. Fails on left-recursive grammars.
Bottom-up: Reads input words, tries to match RHS of rules. Risk: builds constituents that are never part of a complete parse.
Chart parsing (Earley): Combines both. Maintains an agenda of active/passive arcs. Uses dynamic programming to avoid recomputation. Handles left-recursion, ambiguity, and runs in \(O(n^3)\).
Best: Chart parsing — only method that guarantees efficiency and completeness for general CFGs.
