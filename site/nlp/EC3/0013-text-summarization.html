1 · What Is Text Summarization?

Analogy

You read 10 news articles about an earthquake and write a 3-sentence briefing for your boss. That's text summarization — reducing a large document to its essential points without losing critical information.

Definition

Text summarization is the task of producing a shorter version of one or more documents that preserves the most important information. The output can be a subset of original sentences (extractive) or newly generated text (abstractive).

Applications

  • Outlines or abstracts of documents and articles
  • Chat and email thread summaries
  • Action items from meetings
  • News aggregation and briefings
  • Question answering (create answer from multiple documents)
  • Simplifying technical content for non-expert audiences

2 · Types of Summarization

DimensionType 1Type 2
By methodExtractive: Selects sentences from source verbatimAbstractive: Generates new text paraphrasing the source
By inputSingle-document: One source documentMulti-document: Multiple articles on same topic
By focusGeneric: Most important overall contentQuery-focused: Most relevant to a specific question
Extractive vs Abstractive Example

Original: "Heavy rainfall occurred continuously for three days in Hyderabad. Several roads were flooded, public transport was disrupted, and schools remained closed. The municipal authorities deployed rescue teams and provided temporary shelters."

Extractive: "Several roads were flooded, public transport was disrupted, and schools remained closed. The municipal authorities deployed rescue teams and provided temporary shelters."

Abstractive: "Continuous rainfall caused severe flooding in Hyderabad, disrupting daily life. Authorities responded with rescue operations and temporary shelters."

Exam Note

Extractive is simpler but may be incoherent when sentences from different parts are joined. Abstractive is harder (requires NLG) but produces more natural, coherent summaries. Neural models (T5, BART) can do abstractive summarization end-to-end.

3 · The Three-Stage Pipeline

  1. Content Selection: Decide which sentences/facts are most important
  2. Information Ordering: Determine the best order to present them
  3. Sentence Realization: Clean up, merge, re-reference, and edit sentences

Information Ordering Strategies

  • Chronological: Order by document date (for news summaries)
  • Coherence: Place semantically similar sentences adjacent (cosine similarity between consecutive sentences maximized)
  • Entity-based: Sentences discussing the same entity placed together (Barzilay & Lapata 2007)
  • Topical: Group by topic, learn topic ordering from source documents

Sentence Realization Challenges

  • Coreference: "he" might become ambiguous when sentence is removed from context → replace with name
  • Discourse connectives: "However, moreover, subsequently" — removed sentence breaks the chain
  • Compression: Remove redundant clauses from extracted sentences

4 · Content Selection Methods

Frequency-Based (Baseline)

Greedy Frequency Method
  1. Compute word probability from input: \(p(w) = \frac{\text{count}(w)}{\sum_{w'}\text{count}(w')}\)
  2. Score each sentence as sum of word probabilities
  3. Pick the highest-scoring sentence
  4. Repeat (but penalize sentences that overlap with already-selected ones)

Insight: The topic of a document gets repeated many times. Repeated words → high probability → high sentence score.

TF-IDF Scoring

$$w_{ik} = tf_{ik} \cdot \log\frac{N}{n_k}$$

where \(tf_{ik}\) = frequency of term \(k\) in document \(i\), \(N\) = total documents, \(n_k\) = documents containing term \(k\). IDF down-weights common terms, up-weights discriminative ones.

Lead Baseline

Simple but Surprisingly Strong Baseline

Take the first N sentences of the document. For news articles (inverted pyramid structure), this is hard to beat — journalists put the most important information first. Always compute the lead baseline before evaluating a new summarizer.

5 · Maximal Marginal Relevance (MMR)

The Redundancy Problem

Simple frequency selection tends to pick multiple sentences saying the same thing. MMR addresses this by jointly optimizing for relevance to query and novelty relative to what's already selected.

$$\text{MMR}(d_i) = \lambda \cdot \text{Sim}_1(d_i, q) - (1 - \lambda) \cdot \max_{d_j \in S} \text{Sim}_2(d_i, d_j)$$

Where: \(d_i\) = candidate sentence, \(q\) = query, \(S\) = already-selected sentences, \(\lambda \in [0,1]\) balances relevance vs novelty.

MMR Algorithm (Greedy)

S = {} -- empty selected set repeat until |S| = desired length: d* = arg max_{d ∉ S} MMR(d, q, S) S = S ∪ {d*} return S in original document order

MMR Worked Example (\(\lambda = 0.5\))

3-Sentence Summary from 5 Candidates

Query similarity: sim(d1,q)=0.9, sim(d2,q)=0.9, sim(d3,q)=0.5, sim(d4,q)=0.1, sim(d5,q)=0.5

Iteration 1: S={} → MMR(di)=0.5×sim(di,q) → max at d1 (0.45). S={d1}.

Iteration 2: sim(d1,d2)=0.1 → MMR(d2) = 0.5×0.9 − 0.5×0.1 = 0.39. sim(d1,d5)=small → MMR(d5)=0.19. Best: d2. S={d1,d2}.

Iteration 3: For d3: max{sim(d1,d3)=0.23, sim(d2,d3)=0.29}=0.29 → MMR(d3)=0.5×0.5−0.5×0.29=0.105. Others lower. Best: d3. Final S={d1,d2,d3}.

6 · LexRank — Graph-Based Summarization

Analogy

LexRank treats sentences like web pages. Important web pages are linked to by many other important pages (PageRank). Important sentences are similar to many other similar sentences — they're the "hub" sentences that best represent the document's core topic.

  1. Split document into sentences
  2. Convert each sentence to a TF-IDF or embedding vector
  3. Compute cosine similarity between every pair of sentences
  4. Create a graph: nodes = sentences; edges = pairs with similarity ≥ threshold
  5. Apply PageRank to find the most "central" sentences
  6. Select top-ranked sentences; preserve original order in summary
PageRank centrality: $$p(v_i) = \frac{1-d}{n} + d \sum_{v_j: e_{ij} \in E} \frac{p(v_j)}{\text{deg}(v_j)}$$

where \(d\) = damping factor (~0.85), \(n\) = number of sentences.

LexRank vs MMR
  • LexRank: Global graph centrality — finds sentences important relative to the whole document. No redundancy control by default.
  • MMR: Greedy, iterative — explicitly penalizes redundancy. Better for diverse summaries. Requires a query.

7 · ROUGE Evaluation

ROUGE — Recall-Oriented Understudy for Gisting Evaluation

ROUGE measures automatic summarization quality by comparing the system summary to human reference summaries via n-gram overlap.

MetricMeasuresFormula
ROUGE-NN-gram recall\(\frac{\sum_{gram_n \in \text{ref}} \text{Count}_\text{match}(gram_n)}{\sum_{gram_n \in \text{ref}} \text{Count}(gram_n)}\)
ROUGE-1Unigram overlapWord-level recall
ROUGE-2Bigram overlapMore sensitive to word order
ROUGE-LLongest common subsequenceCaptures sentence-level structure
ROUGE-1 Worked Example

Reference: "The cat sat on the mat"
System: "The cat is on the mat"
Matching unigrams: {the, cat, on, the, mat} → 5 matches out of 6 reference words
ROUGE-1 Recall = 5/6 ≈ 0.83
ROUGE-1 Precision = 5/6 ≈ 0.83 (5 matches in 6 system words)
ROUGE-1 F1 = 0.83

ROUGE Limitations
  • Focuses on recall — doesn't penalize long summaries enough
  • Surface-level: "car" and "automobile" don't match (use ROUGE-BE or embedding variants)
  • Correlates better with extractive than abstractive summaries
  • Always report ROUGE-1, ROUGE-2, ROUGE-L for completeness

Supervised Content Selection

Train a binary classifier: for each sentence, predict "should this be included in the summary?" Features: sentence position, length, TF-IDF score, presence of named entities, discourse markers.

Exam Note: Lead Baseline Beats Many Systems

For news summarization, the simple lead baseline (first 3 sentences) is often hard to beat on ROUGE. This is because news articles follow the inverted pyramid structure — most important info first. Any paper on news summarization must compare against this baseline.

10 · Practice Questions

Q1 · What is the difference between extractive and abstractive summarization?

Extractive: Selects and concatenates sentences directly from the source document. Guaranteed grammaticality of individual sentences; may be incoherent as a whole; cannot rephrase.
Abstractive: Generates new text paraphrasing the source. Can condense, merge, and rephrase; more coherent; risk of hallucination or factual errors.
Modern systems (BART, T5) do abstractive; most production systems use hybrid (extractive pre-selection + abstractive generation).

Q2 · Explain the three stages of the summarization pipeline.

Stage 1 — Content Selection: Identify which sentences/facts to include. Methods: frequency (TF-IDF), graph centrality (LexRank), MMR, supervised classifiers.
Stage 2 — Information Ordering: Determine sequence. Methods: chronological (date of document), coherence (maximize cosine between consecutive sentences), entity-based, topical.
Stage 3 — Sentence Realization: Edit sentences for the new context: resolve coreference ("he" → "Obama"), remove broken discourse connectives ("However" at the start), compress sentences to remove redundant clauses.

Q3 · Compute ROUGE-2 for: Reference = "a b c d", System = "a b e d".

Reference bigrams: {(a,b), (b,c), (c,d)} → 3 bigrams. System bigrams: {(a,b), (b,e), (e,d)} → 3 bigrams. Matching: {(a,b)} → 1 match. ROUGE-2 Recall = 1/3 ≈ 0.33. ROUGE-2 Precision = 1/3 ≈ 0.33. ROUGE-2 F1 = 0.33.

Q4 (6-mark) · Compare MMR and LexRank for multi-document summarization.

MMR (Maximal Marginal Relevance): Greedy iterative selection. Each step picks the sentence maximizing λ·sim(d,q) − (1−λ)·max_j sim(d,d_j). Explicitly balances relevance (to query) and novelty (relative to selected set). Requires a query. Good for query-focused summarization. O(n²) pairwise comparisons per step.
LexRank: Graph-based global approach. Builds sentence similarity graph; applies PageRank to rank by "centrality" — sentences similar to many others score high. No query needed. Good for generic summarization. Can rank all sentences in one pass. May not control redundancy unless combined with coverage constraints.
Comparison: MMR is better at avoiding redundancy (by design); LexRank is better at finding globally important sentences across a collection. In practice, LexRank combined with MMR-style redundancy penalty gives best results for multi-document generic summarization.
