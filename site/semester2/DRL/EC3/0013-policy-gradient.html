Why Policy Gradient?

Everything up to now — TD learning, Q-learning, value approximation — works by estimating a value function and deriving a policy from it. That works well in discrete action spaces. But two important real-world cases break it.

Two scenarios where value-based methods struggle

Continuous actions: A robotic arm must choose a torque anywhere in \([-10, 10]\) Nm. There is no "argmax over actions" — there are infinitely many.

Stochastic policies: In poker, the optimal play is to bluff sometimes — a mixed strategy. A deterministic greedy policy (always pick the highest-Q action) is easy to exploit. You need a learned distribution over actions.

Value-based methods also suffer indirectly: small changes in \(Q\) can flip the greedy action dramatically, causing instability. If we instead parameterise the policy directly and update it smoothly, we get stable, differentiable learning.

The policy gradient idea in one sentence

Instead of learning \(Q(s,a)\) and extracting a policy, we directly parameterise \(\pi_\theta(a \mid s)\) — a distribution over actions — and gradient-ascend the expected return \(J(\theta)\) with respect to \(\theta\).

The table below shows which problems each family handles well.

RequirementValue-Based (Q-Learning, DQN)Policy Gradient
Discrete actionsYes — argmax is cheapYes
Continuous actionsHard — argmax is intractableYes — directly outputs distribution
Stochastic policyNot natural — needs extra tricksYes — policy is a distribution by design
Smooth policy updatesargmax causes discontinuitiesGradient steps are smooth
Sample efficiencyGenerally betterOften worse (high variance)

Objective Function & Policy Gradient Theorem

We want to find parameters \(\theta\) that maximise the expected return under policy \(\pi_\theta\):

$$ J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\pi_\theta}\!\left[G_t\right] = \mathbb{E}_{\pi_\theta}\!\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty} \gamma^t R_{t+1}\right] $$

To use gradient ascent — \(\theta \leftarrow \theta + \alpha \nabla_\theta J(\theta)\) — we need \(\nabla_\theta J(\theta)\). The problem is that \(J\) depends on \(\theta\) in two places: through the actions chosen and through the state distribution visited. The Policy Gradient Theorem gives us a clean expression.

Policy Gradient Theorem

$$ \nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\pi_\theta}\!\Bigl[\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(A_t \mid S_t) \cdot Q^{\pi_\theta}(S_t, A_t)\Bigr] $$

We never need to differentiate the state distribution — it cancels out, leaving only the log-derivative of the policy times the action value.

The log-derivative trick (REINFORCE trick)

Why \(\log \pi\)? Notice:

$$ \nabla_\theta \pi_\theta(a \mid s) = \pi_\theta(a \mid s) \cdot \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a \mid s) $$

This lets us write the gradient as an expectation under the policy — which we can estimate by sampling trajectories. No integration needed.

Intuition — the score function

\(\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a \mid s)\) is called the score function. It points in the direction of parameter space that makes action \(a\) in state \(s\) more likely. Multiplying by \(Q^{\pi}(s,a)\) means: move in the direction that increases the probability of the good actions and decreases the probability of bad ones.

Concretely, the gradient update rule is:

$$ \theta \leftarrow \theta + \alpha \cdot \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(A_t \mid S_t) \cdot Q^{\pi}(S_t, A_t) $$

REINFORCE Algorithm

REINFORCE is the simplest policy gradient algorithm. It uses the actual sampled return \(G_t\) as an unbiased estimate of \(Q^{\pi}(s,a)\). No critic, no bootstrapping — just Monte Carlo returns.

Update rule

$$ \theta \leftarrow \theta + \alpha \cdot G_t \cdot \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(A_t \mid S_t) $$
Why G_t is an unbiased estimate of Q

By definition, \(Q^{\pi}(s,a) = \mathbb{E}_\pi[G_t \mid S_t=s, A_t=a]\). So \(G_t\) is a single-sample, unbiased estimate of \(Q^\pi\). The expectation over many trajectories recovers the true gradient.

Full REINFORCE pseudocode

Input: differentiable policy π(a|s,θ), step size α
Initialise θ arbitrarily

Loop forever (for each episode):
  Generate trajectory: S₀,A₀,R₁,S₁,A₁,R₂,…,S_{T-1},A_{T-1},R_T
                   ← using π(·|·,θ)
  For each step t = 0,1,…,T−1:
    G ← Σ_{k=t+1}^{T} γ^{k−t−1} R_k  ← actual return from t
    θ ← θ + α · G · ∇_θ log π(A_t|S_t,θ)
Key limitation — high variance

REINFORCE is unbiased but has extremely high variance. \(G_t\) can swing wildly between trajectories even for the same state-action pair. A lucky trajectory can push up probabilities of actions that were actually mediocre. This makes learning slow and unstable.

Analogy — grading by final score only

Imagine training a student by showing them the final exam score and saying "do more of whatever you did on this attempt." If the score was 80, you credit every choice in the session — including toilet breaks and doodles — not just the studying. High-variance feedback.

Baseline & the Advantage Function

The fix to REINFORCE's high variance is to subtract a baseline \(b(s)\) from the return. This does not bias the gradient but dramatically reduces its variance.

Why subtracting a baseline is safe

$$ \mathbb{E}_{\pi}\!\Bigl[\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a \mid s) \cdot b(s)\Bigr] = 0 $$

Any baseline \(b(s)\) that does not depend on the action is unbiased — its expectation under the policy is exactly zero. So we can subtract it for free:

$$ \theta \leftarrow \theta + \alpha \cdot \bigl(G_t - b(S_t)\bigr) \cdot \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(A_t \mid S_t) $$

The natural choice: the value function

The best baseline to minimise variance is \(b(s) = V^\pi(s)\). When we subtract it from \(Q\), we get the advantage function:

$$ A^\pi(s, a) = Q^\pi(s, a) - V^\pi(s) $$
What the advantage function measures

\(A^\pi(s,a)\) answers: "How much better is action \(a\) compared to the average action in state \(s\)?"

If \(A > 0\): action \(a\) is better than average — increase its probability.
If \(A < 0\): action \(a\) is worse than average — decrease its probability.
If \(A = 0\): action \(a\) is exactly average — no change needed.

Why variance drops

Without baseline: a return of \(+50\) in a state where the average is \(+48\) gets credited the full \(+50\). With baseline: only the excess \(+2\) matters. Returns fluctuate around the mean; the advantage fluctuates far less.

Actor-Critic (A2C)

REINFORCE waits for the full episode to compute \(G_t\). Actor-Critic replaces \(G_t\) with a bootstrapped estimate — the TD error — evaluated by a separate critic network. This gets the best of both worlds: lower variance from bootstrapping, lower bias from learning a value function.

Two networks, two jobs
  • Actor \(\pi_\theta(a \mid s)\): chooses actions. Parameters \(\theta\).
  • Critic \(V_w(s)\): estimates the state value. Parameters \(w\).

The critic's TD error \(\delta\) serves as an estimate of the advantage:

\[\delta = R_{t+1} + \gamma V_w(S_{t+1}) - V_w(S_t) \approx A^\pi(S_t, A_t)\]

Analogy — student and coach

The actor is the student making decisions. The critic is the coach watching and saying "that move was better than I expected" (\(\delta > 0\)) or "worse" (\(\delta < 0\)). The student adjusts probabilities accordingly — without waiting for the full game to end.

A2C pseudocode (one-step)

Initialise θ (actor), w (critic)

Loop for each episode:
  Observe S
  Loop for each step:
    A ~ π(·|S,θ)  ← sample action from actor
    Take A; observe R, S′
    δ ← R + γ·V_w(S′) − V_w(S)  ← TD error = advantage estimate
    w ← w + α_w · δ · ∇_w V_w(S)  ← update critic
    θ ← θ + α_θ · δ · ∇_θ log π(A|S,θ)  ← update actor
    S ← S′
  Until S is terminal

The key difference from REINFORCE: updates happen every single step, not at episode end. The critic's estimate \(\delta\) replaces the raw return \(G_t\).

Exam tip — two learning rates

Actor-Critic has two separate learning rates: \(\alpha_\theta\) for the policy parameters and \(\alpha_w\) for the value parameters. They can be tuned independently. The critic usually converges faster, so \(\alpha_w > \alpha_\theta\) is common.

PPO — Proximal Policy Optimisation

Standard policy gradient methods have a fragility problem: too large a gradient step can move the policy so far that performance collapses — and you can never recover, because the new policy collects different data. PPO fixes this with a clipped surrogate objective that prevents dangerously large updates.

The probability ratio

Let \(\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}\) be the policy used to collect data. The ratio of the new policy to the old one is:

$$ r_t(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(A_t \mid S_t)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(A_t \mid S_t)} $$

If \(r_t = 1\): policy hasn't changed. If \(r_t = 2\): new policy is twice as likely to take that action. PPO constrains \(r_t\) to stay near 1.

The clipped surrogate objective

$$ L^{\text{CLIP}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_t\!\Bigl[\min\!\bigl(r_t(\theta)\,A_t,\;\operatorname{clip}(r_t(\theta),\,1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon)\,A_t\bigr)\Bigr] $$
How clipping works — two cases

When \(A_t > 0\) (action was good): we want to increase \(r_t\), but we cap the gain at \(r_t = 1+\varepsilon\). Going further gives no extra reward signal — discouraging over-enthusiastic increases.

When \(A_t < 0\) (action was bad): we want to decrease \(r_t\), but we stop the decrease at \(r_t = 1-\varepsilon\). Going further gives no extra signal — the update is done.

Typical \(\varepsilon = 0.2\): the policy is allowed to change probabilities by at most ±20% before the clipping engages.

Analogy — a speed limiter for learning

Imagine you're adjusting a recipe. Standard PG says "this dish was great, add 10× as much salt next time." PPO says "it was great — add a bit more salt, but no more than 20% of what you currently use." You still improve, but you don't overshoot into inedible territory.

REINFORCE vs A2C vs PPO — Comparison

All three are policy gradient methods. They differ in how they estimate the gradient and how aggressively they update.

PropertyREINFORCEA2C (Actor-Critic)PPO
Gradient estimate Full return \(G_t\) (Monte Carlo) TD error \(\delta\) (one-step bootstrap) Clipped surrogate \(L^{\text{CLIP}}\)
Variance Very high Medium (lower than REINFORCE) Low (clipping + advantage norm)
Bias Zero (unbiased) Some (bootstrap introduces bias) Some (same as A2C, plus clipping)
Training stability Low — large variance swings Medium High — clipping prevents collapse
Sample efficiency Low — on-policy, one use per sample Low–medium Higher — multiple epochs per batch
Continuous actions Yes Yes Yes
Needs critic network No — policy only Yes Yes
Typical use Simple / educational baseline Parallel environments (A3C variant) Production RL; RLHF for LLMs
The evolution in one line

REINFORCE → add baseline → Actor-Critic (add critic) → PPO (add clipping + multiple update epochs per batch). Each step reduces variance or improves stability at a small cost in bias or complexity.

Exam tips
  • REINFORCE update: \(\theta \leftarrow \theta + \alpha G_t \nabla \log \pi\). Full return, episodic only.
  • Advantage: \(A = Q - V\). Always subtract state value (not action value) as baseline.
  • A2C TD error \(\delta = R + \gamma V(S') - V(S)\) is an approximation of the advantage, not the exact advantage.
  • PPO clip: unclipped when \(r_t \in [1-\varepsilon, 1+\varepsilon]\); clipped (flat) outside. \(\varepsilon = 0.2\) by default.
  • PPO is the algorithm behind RLHF (fine-tuning LLMs from human feedback).
