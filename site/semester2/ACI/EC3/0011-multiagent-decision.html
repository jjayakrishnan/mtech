1. Why Single-Agent AI Is Not Enough

Most AI textbook algorithms assume the environment stays still while the agent acts. The environment does not move, respond, or scheme.

That assumption collapses in many real settings. Here are four where it breaks completely.

  • Chess: Every move you make changes what your opponent will do next. An opponent who plays optimally will exploit any predictable strategy.
  • Autonomous driving: Every car on the road makes independent decisions simultaneously. One vehicle braking affects dozens of others.
  • Online auctions: Each bid by one participant changes the rational bid for every other participant.
  • Cybersecurity: Attackers continuously adapt to whatever defences are deployed. A firewall rule known to an attacker is an invitation.
Everyday picture — chess vs. solitaire

Playing solitaire is a single-agent problem: the cards do not respond to your moves. Playing chess is a multi-agent problem: your opponent observes every move and adjusts. The algorithms that solve solitaire fail at chess precisely because the environment has agency.

The three questions Game Theory answers

Game Theory is the mathematical study of strategic decision making among multiple rational agents whose outcomes depend on all participants' choices.

The three fundamental questions
  • What should I do? — which action maximises my expected outcome?
  • What will my opponent do? — what is rational for them, given what I might do?
  • What is the best possible response? — given my prediction of their action, what do I do?

Each question feeds the next. The circular dependency is what makes multi-agent reasoning hard and interesting.

Exam tip — why it's not just optimisation

Single-agent problems have a fixed objective function. Multi-agent problems have coupled objective functions — my payoff depends on your choice, and yours depends on mine. Standard optimisation ignores this coupling; Game Theory embraces it.

2. Five Elements of a Game

Any strategic interaction can be described by exactly five components. Know these cold.

1 — Players

The players are the decision makers in the game. They may be human, AI agents, companies, robots, or nations. The number of players is written \(n\), with the set \(N = \{1, 2, \ldots, n\}\).

Example: in an online auction, each bidder is a player.

2 — Actions

The actions (or moves) are the choices available to a player at any decision point. Player \(i\)'s action set is written \(A_i\). In chess, the legal moves at each position form the action set.

3 — Strategy

A strategy is a complete plan: it specifies which action a player takes in every possible situation they might face. A single action is not a strategy. A strategy covers all contingencies.

Strategy vs. action — the chess analogy

Playing "e4" on move 1 is an action. Having a plan that covers every possible reply by your opponent — including moves you hope never to see — is a strategy. A chess strategy is a branching decision tree, not a single move.

4 — Payoff (Utility)

The payoff is the numerical reward a player receives when the game ends. Payoffs depend on the combination of strategies chosen by all players, not just on one player's choice.

Key point: two players can choose the same action but receive different payoffs, because payoff depends on the entire outcome, not just one player's move.

5 — Information

The information is what a player knows before making a decision. There are three regimes:

  • Complete information: all players know the full payoff structure.
  • Partial information: players know their own payoffs but not others'.
  • Imperfect information: players cannot observe all previous actions (e.g. poker — you cannot see opponents' cards).

3. Rational Agents in Game Theory

Game Theory's core assumption is that every player is rational. This is a technical definition, not a moral one.

Formal definition of rationality

A rational agent selects actions that maximise its expected utility, given its beliefs about what other players will do. Specifically, a rational agent:

  • knows the rules of the game
  • knows all available actions
  • prefers higher utility to lower utility
  • predicts other players' likely actions
  • plans several steps ahead
  • never intentionally selects a worse outcome when a better one exists

Rational does not mean selfish

This surprises many students. A rational player may cooperate if cooperation results in higher payoff than defecting. Cooperation is rational whenever it pays better. Selfishness is a value judgement; rationality is about consistent utility maximisation.

Everyday picture — two cafes on the same street

Two cafes both set their prices simultaneously. Each wants to maximise its own profit. That makes both rational. But if they figure out that matching prices at £3 earns each £500/day, while undercutting leads to a price war leaving both at £200/day, the rational choice may be to cooperate on price — not because they are nice, but because cooperation pays more.

Common knowledge of rationality

Standard game theory also assumes common knowledge of rationality: not only is each player rational, each player knows the others are rational, and each player knows that the others know this, and so on. This assumption lets each player predict what a rational opponent will do.

Exam trap — bounded rationality

Real humans are not perfectly rational — they tire, forget, and use shortcuts. Bounded rationality (Herbert Simon) captures the idea that real agents optimise subject to cognitive limits. Full rationality is a modelling assumption in classical game theory, not a description of reality.

4. Types of Games

Four dichotomies classify almost every game you will encounter.

Zero-sum vs non-zero-sum

In a zero-sum game, one player's gain exactly equals another player's loss. Total payoff is constant — wealth is transferred, never created or destroyed.

  • Examples: chess, poker (without rake), tic-tac-toe, penalty kicks.

In a non-zero-sum game, both players can gain (or both can lose). Trade negotiation is the canonical example: a mutually beneficial deal makes both parties richer.

  • Examples: trade agreements, climate accords, traffic management, the Prisoner's Dilemma.

Cooperative vs non-cooperative

In a cooperative game, players can communicate, form binding agreements, and coordinate strategy before and during play. The focus is on which coalitions form and how they share the gains.

In a non-cooperative game, players act independently. Agreements are not binding. Each player must decide unilaterally, anticipating what others will do.

Simultaneous vs sequential

In simultaneous games, all players choose their actions at the same time (or without knowing others' choices). The payoff matrix captures these games.

In sequential games, players move one after another. Earlier players can observe some or all of the previous actions before deciding. Game trees model these.

Perfect vs imperfect information

In a perfect information game, every player knows the complete history of all moves. Chess is the canonical example.

In an imperfect information game, some information is hidden. Poker is the canonical example — you cannot see opponents' hands.

5. Normal Form (Strategic Form) — The Payoff Matrix

The normal form represents a simultaneous game as a matrix. Each row is one strategy of Player 1; each column is one strategy of Player 2. Each cell holds the payoff pair \((u_1, u_2)\) for that combination of strategies.

Formal definition

A game in normal form is a triple \((N, S, u)\) where:

  • \(N = \{1, 2, \ldots, n\}\) — set of players
  • \(S = S_1 \times S_2 \times \cdots \times S_n\) — strategy profiles (one strategy per player)
  • \(u = (u_1, u_2, \ldots, u_n)\) — payoff function; \(u_i(s)\) is player \(i\)'s payoff for profile \(s\)

Reading a payoff matrix

Convention: the first number in each cell is Player 1's payoff; the second is Player 2's. Player 1 picks rows; Player 2 picks columns.

B plays B1B plays B2
A plays A1(3, 1)(−1, 2)
A plays A2(2, 0)(4, 3)

Read cell (A2, B2): Player A gets 4, Player B gets 3. This happens when A plays A2 and B plays B2 simultaneously.

Prisoner's Dilemma in normal form

Two suspects are held in separate rooms. Each can either Stay Silent (Cooperate = C) or Confess (Defect = D). They cannot communicate. Payoffs are in years-of-freedom lost (negated so higher is better):

Ben: Silent (C)Ben: Confess (D)
Alan: Silent (C)(−1, −1)(−15, 0)
Alan: Confess (D)(0, −15)(−10, −10)

Read the matrix: if both stay silent, each loses 1 year. If Alan confesses and Ben stays silent, Alan goes free (0) and Ben loses 15 years. If both confess, each loses 10 years.

Exam tip — which payoff is which player?

Always check the convention. In AIMA (Russell & Norvig), payoffs are listed as (row player, column player). If the question uses a different convention, relabel before analysing. Getting this wrong flips every answer.

6. Dominant Strategies and IEDS

A dominant strategy is a strategy that is best for you regardless of what your opponent does. You do not need to predict the opponent — you just play it.

Strict dominance

Strategy \(s_i\) strictly dominates strategy \(s_i'\) for player \(i\) if, for every strategy profile \(s_{-i}\) of all other players:

$$u_i(s_i,\ s_{-i}) > u_i(s_i',\ s_{-i})$$

In plain English: \(s_i\) always gives a higher payoff than \(s_i'\), no matter what others do.

Weak dominance

Strategy \(s_i\) weakly dominates \(s_i'\) if it does at least as well in every scenario and strictly better in at least one.

Finding dominant strategies — worked example

Consider this payoff matrix (P1's payoff only shown, for clarity):

P2: LeftP2: Right
P1: Up42
P1: Down63

Compare P1's rows column by column:

  • If P2 plays Left: Up gives 4, Down gives 6. Down wins.
  • If P2 plays Right: Up gives 2, Down gives 3. Down wins.

Down strictly dominates Up for P1. A rational P1 will always play Down, regardless of P2's choice.

Iterated Elimination of Dominated Strategies (IEDS)

IEDS removes dominated strategies one by one. After each removal, the game shrinks. Repeat until no more dominated strategies remain. Whatever survives is the set of rationalizable outcomes.

IEDS worked example — 3×3 game

Payoff pairs (P1, P2):

LMR
T(4,3)(5,1)(6,2)
M(2,1)(8,4)(3,6)
B(3,0)(9,6)(2,8)

Step 1: Does P1 have a dominated row? Compare T, M, B column by column.
Column L: T=4, M=2, B=3 → T best. Column M: T=5, M=8, B=9 → B best. Column R: T=6, M=3, B=2 → T best.
No row is dominated by another single row. Continue to P2.

Step 2: For P2, compare L, M, R row by row.
Row T: L=3, M=1, R=2 → L best. Row M: L=1, M=4, R=6 → R best. Row B: L=0, M=6, R=8 → R best.
Column L is dominated by R in rows M and B (L gives 1,0 vs R giving 6,8). But not in row T. So L is not strictly dominated. No column is eliminated in step 2 either.
(This particular game requires mixed strategy analysis to proceed further.)

Why IEDS works — the logic of rational opponents

If a strategy is dominated, a rational player never plays it. And if it's common knowledge that no rational player plays dominated strategies, we can remove those strategies and repeat. The surviving strategies are what rational players with rational opponents would consider playing.

7. Nash Equilibrium

A Nash Equilibrium (NE) is a strategy profile where no single player can improve their own payoff by switching to a different strategy, holding all other players' strategies fixed.

Formal definition

A strategy profile \((s_1^*, s_2^*, \ldots, s_n^*)\) is a Nash Equilibrium if, for every player \(i\) and every alternative strategy \(s_i \in S_i\):

$$u_i(s_i^*,\ s_{-i}^*) \geq u_i(s_i,\ s_{-i}^*)$$

where \(s_{-i}^* = (s_1^*, \ldots, s_{i-1}^*, s_{i+1}^*, \ldots, s_n^*)\) denotes all other players' equilibrium strategies.

Intuition — the "no regret" point

Imagine everyone announces their strategy. Then everyone sees all strategies at once. At a Nash Equilibrium, nobody wants to change their mind given what they now know about everyone else. It's a stable resting point. Not necessarily the best outcome for everyone — just the point where unilateral deviation doesn't help.

Finding NE in a 2×2 matrix

The method: for each cell, check if either player would benefit from switching. If neither benefits from switching, mark it as an NE.

Alternatively, use the underlining method:

  1. For each column, underline the row player's best payoff.
  2. For each row, underline the column player's best payoff.
  3. A cell where both payoffs are underlined is a Nash Equilibrium.

Mixed strategy Nash Equilibrium

Pure strategy NE may not always exist. A mixed strategy assigns probabilities to each pure strategy. Player \(i\) randomises with probability \(p\) on action A and \(1-p\) on action B. Nash's theorem guarantees that every finite game has at least one NE in mixed strategies.

At a mixed strategy NE, each player must be indifferent between the strategies they mix over — otherwise they would not randomise.

8. The Prisoner's Dilemma — Deep Dive

The Prisoner's Dilemma is the most studied game in all of game theory. It captures why rational individuals often fail to achieve collectively good outcomes.

The setup

Alan and Ben are arrested for the same crime and held in separate rooms. Each can:

  • Cooperate (C) / Stay Silent: don't testify against the other.
  • Defect (D) / Confess: testify against the other in exchange for leniency.

The payoffs (in years of freedom lost; higher = worse; we use utility where higher utility = fewer years lost):

Ben: C (Silent)Ben: D (Confess)
Alan: C (Silent)(−1, −1)(−15, 0)
Alan: D (Confess)(0, −15)(−10, −10)

Why rational agents defect — the dominant strategy argument

Alan's reasoning (Ben's is symmetric)

Case 1 — Suppose Ben stays silent: Alan can stay silent (−1 year) or confess (0 years, i.e., go free). Confessing is better.
Case 2 — Suppose Ben confesses: Alan can stay silent (−15 years) or confess (−10 years). Confessing is better.
Conclusion: Regardless of Ben's choice, Alan is always better off confessing. Confess is a dominant strategy for Alan. By symmetry it is also dominant for Ben. Both confess. Both get −10.

The outcome (Defect, Defect) is the Nash Equilibrium — neither player wants to unilaterally switch, because switching to Silent while the other Defects gives −15. But (Silent, Silent) gives (−1, −1), which is better for both than (−10, −10). The NE is not Pareto optimal.

Social cost and real-world parallels

The dilemma captures a fundamental tension: individual rationality leads to collective irrationality. Real-world versions include:

  • Climate agreements: each country benefits from others reducing emissions while it emits freely, but if all countries reason this way, global warming accelerates.
  • Price wars: each company benefits from undercutting a competitor, but if both undercut, both end up with lower prices and lower profits.
  • Arms races: each nation arms because the other might arm, ending in mutual insecurity at great cost.
  • Ad blocking vs. ad revenue: users install ad blockers; websites retaliate with paywalls; both end up worse off.

Exam trap — "NE is optimal" is wrong

Nash Equilibrium is the stable outcome, not necessarily the best outcome. In the Prisoner's Dilemma, (D,D) is the NE but (C,C) Pareto-dominates it. This is the whole point of the dilemma. Always distinguish between NE, Pareto optimality, and social welfare maximisation.

9. Extensive Form — Game Trees and Backward Induction

Payoff matrices capture simultaneous games well. Sequential games — where players move one after another — need a different representation: the game tree (extensive form).

Components of an extensive-form game
  • Decision nodes: points where a player must choose an action.
  • Branches: the available actions at each decision node.
  • Terminal nodes (leaves): end states where payoffs are assigned.
  • Information sets: groups of nodes a player cannot distinguish between (captures imperfect information).
  • Payoff vectors: one payoff per player at each terminal node.

Backward Induction

To solve a sequential game, start at the leaves and work backward toward the root. At each node, the player whose turn it is picks the branch with the highest payoff for them. Replace the subtree with that payoff and continue up.

Everyday picture — planning a meal

You are deciding whether to cook or order takeaway. If you cook, your housemate decides whether to help or not. If they help, the meal is good (you: 4, them: 3); if they don't, it's mediocre (you: 1, them: 5 — less effort). If you order takeaway, both get moderate satisfaction (you: 3, them: 2). Backward induction: your housemate, faced with cook, will not help (5 > 3). So you predict cook → mediocre. That gives you 1. Ordering gives you 3. Rational choice: order takeaway.

Subgame Perfect Nash Equilibrium (SPNE)

Standard NE can include non-credible threats — commitments a player would not actually follow through on. SPNE eliminates these by requiring NE in every subgame (every subtree of the full game tree).

Backward induction finds the unique SPNE in games with perfect information and finitely many stages.

Exam tip — NE vs SPNE

A Nash Equilibrium in a sequential game may rely on threats that would never be rational to carry out. SPNE rules these out by requiring optimality at every node, not just along the equilibrium path. Backward induction always finds SPNE, not merely NE.

10. Repeated Games — When the Shadow of the Future Cooperates

The Prisoner's Dilemma played once leads to mutual defection. But in many real situations, the same players interact over and over. Repeated games change the strategic landscape dramatically.

One-shot vs. repeated

PropertyOne-shot gameRepeated game
Number of rounds1Multiple (finite or infinite)
HistoryNoneAll previous actions known
ReputationIrrelevantCritical
Cooperation in PDCannot sustainCan sustain with right strategy

Common strategies in repeated games

Grim Trigger: Cooperate until the opponent defects. Once they defect, defect forever. The most severe punishment — cooperation is never restored.

Tit-for-Tat: Start by cooperating. Then copy the opponent's previous move. Cooperate if they cooperated last round; defect if they defected last round.

Why Tit-for-Tat works so well

Tit-for-Tat won Robert Axelrod's famous computer tournament (1980) in the infinitely repeated Prisoner's Dilemma. It succeeds because it is:

  • Nice: never defects first — starts cooperating, avoiding unnecessary conflict.
  • Retaliatory: immediately punishes defection — the opponent cannot exploit it.
  • Forgiving: returns to cooperation as soon as the opponent cooperates again.
  • Transparent: the strategy is simple to understand, making cooperation easy to establish.

Hawk strategy: Always defect, regardless of history. Maximises short-term gain; destroys long-term cooperation.

Dove strategy: Always cooperate. Can be exploited by a Hawk opponent.

Tat-for-Tit: The mirror image of Tit-for-Tat — starts by defecting, then copies the opponent. Less common; useful in environments where deception is prevalent.

Everyday picture — two autonomous delivery robots

Two robots repeatedly share a narrow corridor. If both cooperate (yield turns), traffic flows at full speed. If one behaves selfishly (charges through), the other is stuck. In a one-shot encounter the selfish move pays. But if they meet daily, the selfish robot builds a bad reputation — the cooperative robot starts charging through too. Both suffer. Tit-for-Tat restores cooperation: "I'll yield today, but I remember what you did yesterday."

Exam tip — finite vs. infinite repetition

In a finitely repeated Prisoner's Dilemma with a known end date, backward induction unravels cooperation entirely: both players defect on the last round (no future to care about), so the second-to-last round is also effectively the last, and so on. Cooperation requires either infinite repetition, uncertainty about the end date, or a different game structure.

11. Shapley Values — Fair Division of Cooperative Surplus

Cooperative game theory asks: when a group of agents work together and create a total value, how should that value be divided fairly?

The Shapley value answers this question by asking: "what is each player's average marginal contribution across all possible orderings in which players could join the coalition?"

Characteristic function

A cooperative game is defined by \((N, v)\) where \(N\) is the set of players and \(v: 2^N \to \mathbb{R}\) is the characteristic function. For each subset (coalition) \(S \subseteq N\), \(v(S)\) is the total value that coalition \(S\) can guarantee by itself. \(v(\emptyset) = 0\) always.

The Shapley value formula

$$\phi_i(v) = \sum_{S \subseteq N \setminus \{i\}} \frac{|S|!\,(|N|-|S|-1)!}{|N|!} \bigl[v(S \cup \{i\}) - v(S)\bigr]$$

Each term computes player \(i\)'s marginal contribution \(v(S \cup \{i\}) - v(S)\) when joining coalition \(S\), weighted by the fraction of all orderings in which \(i\) finds exactly the coalition \(S\) already present.

Computing Shapley values — the ordering method

For small \(n\), the easiest computation: enumerate all \(n!\) orderings. For each ordering, compute each player's marginal contribution (what they add to those already present). Average each player's marginal contribution over all orderings.

Worked example — 3 players, A, B, C

Suppose \(N = \{A, B, C\}\) with characteristic function:

Coalition \(S\)\(v(S)\)
\(\{A\}\)1
\(\{B\}\)1
\(\{C\}\)1
\(\{A,B\}\)4
\(\{A,C\}\)4
\(\{B,C\}\)4
\(\{A,B,C\}\)9

There are \(3! = 6\) orderings. Let's compute player A's marginal contribution in each:

OrderingCoalition before A joins\(v(S \cup A)\)\(v(S)\)A's contribution
A, B, C\(\emptyset\)101
A, C, B\(\emptyset\)101
B, A, C\(\{B\}\)413
B, C, A\(\{B,C\}\)945
C, A, B\(\{C\}\)413
C, B, A\(\{B,C\}\)945

\(\phi_A = (1+1+3+5+3+5)/6 = 18/6 = 3\)

By symmetry, \(\phi_B = \phi_C = 3\). Total: \(3+3+3 = 9 = v(\{A,B,C\})\). ✓ The values sum to the grand coalition's value — a key property called efficiency.

Shapley axioms

The Shapley value is the unique allocation satisfying four axioms:

  • Efficiency: all values sum to \(v(N)\) — nothing is left undistributed.
  • Symmetry: if two players contribute identically in every coalition, they receive equal shares.
  • Dummy: a player who contributes 0 to every coalition receives 0.
  • Additivity: the value for a sum of two games equals the sum of values for each game separately.

12. Mechanism Design — Reverse Game Theory

Standard game theory takes the game as given and finds equilibria. Mechanism design goes the other way: given a desired outcome, design the game so that rational agents' self-interested behaviour produces that outcome.

Everyday picture — designing traffic lights

You cannot force drivers to drive safely. But you can design traffic lights, speed cameras, and fines so that the rational choice for every driver (minimise travel time + avoid fines) happens to produce safe, orderly traffic. You have designed a mechanism that channels self-interest into a good collective outcome.

The VCG mechanism

The Vickrey-Clarke-Groves (VCG) mechanism is the canonical solution for allocating goods among agents with private valuations. In a second-price (Vickrey) auction:

  • Each bidder submits a bid equal to their true valuation.
  • The highest bidder wins.
  • The winner pays the second-highest bid, not their own.

This is incentive compatible: the dominant strategy for every bidder is to bid their true value. No strategic shading of bids. The mechanism extracts honest reporting as the equilibrium outcome.

VCG worked example

Three bidders for a laptop: Alice values it at £500, Bob at £350, Carol at £420.

  • In a first-price auction, Alice might bid £421 (just above Carol's guess). That's strategic and wasteful.
  • In a VCG (second-price) auction, Alice bids £500 honestly. She wins. She pays £420 (Carol's bid). Alice's surplus: £80. No incentive to shade her bid in either direction.

Assistance games

An assistance game is a cooperative mechanism design problem where an AI must infer a human's preferences and act to maximise them. The AI cannot read the human's mind — it must learn from observation and feedback.

The challenge is the value alignment problem: how do you design an AI agent whose self-interest (following its reward signal) aligns with human values even in novel situations the designer did not anticipate?

Exam tip — mechanism design vs game theory

In game theory you are a player optimising your own outcome. In mechanism design you are the designer who sets the rules, and the players are the agents. The designer's goal is to choose rules such that rational play by all agents produces the socially desired outcome. Always identify who is designing and who is playing.

13. Multi-Agent Systems in AI

Game theory provides the theoretical bedrock. Modern AI has built several concrete systems on top of it.

Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL)

In MARL, multiple RL agents learn simultaneously in a shared environment. Each agent's reward depends on all agents' actions — the environment is non-stationary from each individual agent's perspective, because the other agents are learning too.

Key challenges not present in single-agent RL:

  • Non-stationarity: as one agent updates its policy, the optimal policy for all other agents changes.
  • Credit assignment: in cooperative tasks, which agent's action caused the good (or bad) outcome?
  • Equilibrium selection: there may be multiple Nash Equilibria — which one will the agents converge to?
  • Emergent behaviour: complex coordinated behaviours can emerge from simple local rules.

AlphaStar — game theory meets deep RL

DeepMind's AlphaStar (2019) reached grandmaster level at StarCraft II, a game with:

  • Imperfect information (fog of war)
  • Real-time simultaneous moves (thousands per minute)
  • Multiple competing agents (Zerg, Protoss, Terran races)
  • A vast action space (over \(10^{26}\) possible game states)

AlphaStar used a population-based training regime — different agents play against each other, forming a diverse portfolio of strategies. This is directly inspired by game theory's notion of mixed strategies: no single strategy dominates a good diverse population.

Emergent behaviour in MARL

OpenAI's hide-and-seek experiment (2019) is a striking example. Agents were given a simple reward signal (hiders: avoid being seen; seekers: find hiders) with no explicit instructions. Over 500 million games, agents discovered:

  1. Hiders learn to build walls.
  2. Seekers learn to use ramps to vault over walls.
  3. Hiders learn to remove ramps.
  4. Seekers learn to surf boxes into blocked enclosures.

Complex tool use emerged from a simple game-theoretic payoff structure — with no human instruction about tools.

Key takeaways — the whole lesson in five bullets
  • Game theory describes strategic interaction among rational agents whose outcomes are coupled.
  • Normal form (payoff matrix) captures simultaneous games; extensive form (game tree) captures sequential games.
  • Nash Equilibrium is the stable outcome where no player benefits from unilateral deviation — not necessarily the best collective outcome.
  • Shapley values fairly distribute cooperative surplus by averaging each player's marginal contribution across all orderings.
  • Mechanism design reverses the problem: design the rules so rational self-interest produces the desired social outcome.
