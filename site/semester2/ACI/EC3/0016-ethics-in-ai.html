1. Why Ethics in AI?

AI systems make decisions that affect people's lives — hiring, credit, medical diagnosis, bail, self-driving. Unlike a calculator, an AI can embed and amplify hidden biases, act opaquely, and pursue proxies for goals rather than the true goal.

The ACI exam (CS16, Q3B sample) asks you to reason about these concerns as a technical issue, not just a philosophical one. Each ethical concept maps to a concrete algorithmic or design choice.

Key Concepts (EC3 Exam Scope)
  • Fairness: What it means for an AI to treat groups equitably
  • Bias: Where unfairness enters and how to detect it
  • Transparency / XAI: How to explain black-box model decisions
  • Alignment: Making AI pursue the true human objective
  • Discount factor γ: A design knob that is also an ethical decision

2. Three Layers of AI Safety

Analogy — Nuclear Safety

A nuclear power plant has three safety systems: the reactor works as designed (alignment), remains safe when something unexpected happens (robustness), and failures can be detected and shut down before they escalate (correctability). AI systems need the same layered defence.

Layer 1 — Alignment

Technology works in its intended use cases, with fairness and without bias. The AI does what we mean, not just what we say. Reward hacking is an alignment failure: a robot told to "maximise cleaning coverage" blocks the room sensors — technically achieving its metric, ethically wrong.

Layer 2 — Robustness

Safe even in unforeseen or adversarial environments. Handles out-of-distribution inputs without catastrophic failure. Example: a COVID mask-detection AI trained only on one demographic fails on others — lack of robustness caused by representation bias in the training data.

Layer 3 — Correctability

Mistakes can be detected and corrected over time. This requires white-box (explainable) models. If you cannot see why a model made a decision, you cannot fix it when it goes wrong.

  • White box: decision trees, linear models, or black boxes wrapped with LIME/SHAP — reasoning is visible and auditable.
  • Black box: deep NNs, LLMs — users trust output without understanding the reasoning. Chain-of-thought reasoning in modern LLMs is a step toward white-box AI.
Exam tip — Layer ordering matters

Alignment must come first — a robust, correctable AI that pursues the wrong goal is still harmful. Get the goal right (alignment), then harden it against edge cases (robustness), then ensure it can be audited and fixed (correctability).

3. FAT — Fairness, Accountability, Transparency

FAT (also: FATML, FATIMA) is the research field studying how AI systems can be made fair, accountable, and transparent. The professor covered it as a teaser for the Sem-3 FATIMA course — here is the exam-level summary.

The Three Pillars
PillarWhat it meansExample concern
FairnessAI must not discriminate based on protected attributes (race, gender, age)Loan approval system denying minorities at higher rates
AccountabilityClear responsibility when AI causes harm — developer? deployer? regulator?Who is liable when an autonomous car injures someone?
TransparencyDecision-making process must be auditable and understandableEU AI Act (2024) mandates transparency for high-risk AI systems
Ethics by Design

Embed ethical constraints from the start of system design — not as an afterthought. Ask "What can go wrong?" before deployment, not after a scandal. Collaborate with ethicists, sociologists, and domain experts who understand the human impact, not just the algorithm. The professor's advice: "Spend a lot of time thinking about data — how it is acquired, how it is labelled, who labelled it, and whether they had any vested interest."

Key insight — bias survives column deletion

If you remove the gender column from training data, the model can still infer gender from correlated features (name, address, activity patterns, disorders more common in one group). Dropping a sensitive column is not sufficient for fairness — you must audit the full feature space and the labels.

4. Moral Machine — Ethics Is Contextual

The Moral Machine Experiment (MIT / Max Planck)

moralmachine.net — a 13-round game presenting trolley-problem scenarios for autonomous vehicles. The car's brakes fail; you choose who is harmed. Over millions of players worldwide, the experiment reveals how ethical judgments vary across cultures, ages, and contexts.

Scenarios include: 3 law-abiding pedestrians vs 3 jaywalkers; elderly vs children; athletes vs ordinary citizens; humans vs pets. There is no "correct" answer — the game shows where you stand relative to the global population.

The Core Problem for AI Design

When a self-driving car is programmed to minimise harm in unavoidable collisions, the value choices embedded in that algorithm are ethical choices — made by engineers, often without explicit deliberation. Moral Machine makes this visible: these choices must be made explicitly by ethicists and regulators, not as emergent side-effects of unconstrained optimisation.

Legal ≠ Ethical: Pedestrians crossing on red are breaking the law but may still deserve protection. An AI that strictly "punishes" illegal behaviour is making a values choice, not a neutral technical decision. Ethics is contextual — not universally defined.

5. Fairness — Three Competing Definitions

Fairness is not a single concept — different stakeholders hold different intuitions, and they can be mathematically incompatible.

TypeFormal DefinitionIntuitionLimitation
Demographic Parity \(P(\hat{Y}{=}1|A{=}0) = P(\hat{Y}{=}1|A{=}1)\) Same proportion of approvals across groups Ignores whether the prediction is correct
Equalised Odds Equal TPR and FPR across groups Same hit and false-alarm rates May require group-specific thresholds
Calibration / Individual Fairness Similar individuals → similar outcomes Treat like cases alike "Similar" is hard to define across domains
Exam Tip

The sample paper asks about "soft AI vs hard AI" and fairness in Q1. Know one concrete example where demographic parity conflicts with equalised odds (e.g. recidivism prediction: if base rates differ between groups, you can't satisfy both simultaneously — proved by Chouldechova 2017).

Analogy

Think of bias as "garbage in, garbage out" — but silently. A biased dataset doesn't raise an error; the model trains happily and produces a high accuracy on the biased test set. The harm emerges when the model meets the real world.

4. Transparency and Explainable AI (XAI)

Deep neural networks are black boxes: they produce decisions without readable reasoning. XAI techniques make them interpretable.

LIME — Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations

How LIME Works
  1. Pick the instance you want to explain (e.g. a loan application that was rejected).
  2. Generate perturbed copies (slightly modified versions of the input).
  3. Ask the black-box model to predict all perturbed copies.
  4. Fit a simple interpretable model (linear regression, decision tree) to the perturbed data, weighted by proximity to the original instance.
  5. Read off the coefficients of the simple model as the explanation.

Result: "This loan was rejected mainly because monthly debt was high (−0.7) and no credit history (−0.4)."

SHAP — SHapley Additive exPlanations

How SHAP Works

Based on Shapley values from cooperative game theory (Lesson 11). Each feature is a "player"; its Shapley value is the average marginal contribution across all possible orderings of features.

  • Theoretically grounded (unique fair allocation)
  • Satisfies: Efficiency, Symmetry, Dummy, Additivity
  • More consistent than LIME across runs
  • Computationally expensive (exponential in # features, approximated with sampling)
LIME vs SHAP
  • LIME: Faster, local (near one point), model-agnostic, less theoretically grounded, can vary between runs
  • SHAP: Slower, global consistency, theoretically principled via game theory, better for feature importance ranking
Connection to EC3 Lesson 11 (Shapley Values)

Shapley values were introduced in Lesson 11 as a fair division mechanism in cooperative games. SHAP reuses the same formula in ML: features are "players", the model's output is the "value" to be divided, and each feature's Shapley value is its fair share of the prediction. This cross-topic link is exam-worthy.

5. AI Alignment — The Specification Problem

Alignment asks: How do we ensure an AI pursues the goals we actually want, not a proxy that correlates with our goals?

Reward Hacking (Goodhart's Law)

"When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure." AI systems optimise their specified reward, not the intended objective.

  • Viral content AI: Optimises for clicks → promotes outrage and misinformation
  • Boat racing AI: Trained to maximise points → circles a point-collecting obstacle instead of finishing the race
  • Healthcare AI: Maximise patient discharge speed → discharges sick patients prematurely
Alignment Approaches
  • RLHF (Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback): Human raters judge AI outputs; their preferences train a reward model that the AI then optimises. Used in ChatGPT/Claude.
  • Constitutional AI: Specify a set of principles; the AI critiques its own outputs against these principles.
  • Corrigibility: Design AI to allow human correction/shutdown; penalise resistance to human intervention.
  • Inverse Reward Design: Infer the true reward from human behaviour rather than specifying it directly.

6. The Discount Factor γ as an Ethical Design Choice

This is the connection between RL (Lesson 13: MDP) and AI ethics. The sample paper Q1d asks: "Explain the significance of discount factor with a suitable plagiarism-free example."

Recall: Discount Factor in MDP

The optimal value function is: \[ V^*(s) = \max_a \left[ R(s,a) + \gamma \sum_{s'} P(s' \mid s, a)\, V^*(s') \right] \] \(\gamma \in [0, 1)\) is the discount factor. Reward at time \(t\) is multiplied by \(\gamma^t\).

What γ Decides
  • \(\gamma = 0\): Fully myopic. The agent ignores all future consequences. Only the immediate reward matters.
  • \(\gamma = 0.9\): Far-sighted. A reward 10 steps away is worth \(0.9^{10} \approx 35\%\) of its face value. The agent plans ahead but discounts distant outcomes.
  • \(\gamma \to 1\): The agent treats all future rewards almost equally. It plans for the very long run.

Worked Ethical Scenario: Medical AI

Scenario: Chemotherapy Dosing AI

An AI decides chemotherapy doses. Immediate reward = tumour shrinkage. Long-term consequence = organ damage from high doses.

  • \(\gamma = 0\) (myopic): Agent maximises tumour shrinkage now. Prescribes the highest possible dose every time. Patient's long-term organ health is ignored → harmful.
  • \(\gamma = 0.95\) (far-sighted): Agent balances short-term tumour reduction with long-term patient survival and quality of life. Recommends lower but sustained doses with rest periods → more ethical.

Key insight: Setting γ is an ethical decision, not just a technical hyperparameter. A low γ makes the AI exploit short-term metrics at the expense of long-term human welfare.

Climate and Policy Parallels

γ in Policy Decisions
  • Carbon emissions AI (\(\gamma = 0\)): Maximises economic output now, ignores climate damage in 20 years.
  • Carbon emissions AI (\(\gamma = 0.99\)): Values future climate nearly as much as present output → recommends early and sustained emission cuts.
  • Education policy AI: Low γ optimises test scores next term; high γ optimises student critical thinking and lifelong income.

7. Model 10-Mark Answer: Q3B — Learning Agents and Ethics

The sample paper Q3B asks about learning agents in the context of ethical AI. Here is a full-mark model answer structure.

Q3B: "Describe the role of a learning agent in ethical AI and the challenges in its design." (10 marks)

Part 1 — What a Learning Agent Does (2 marks)

  • A learning agent improves its performance over time by using feedback from experience (see AIMA Chapter 2).
  • It comprises four components: Performance element (acts), Critic (evaluates), Learning element (updates), Problem generator (suggests exploration).

Part 2 — Why Learning ≠ Ethical by Default (3 marks)

  • Reward hacking: If the reward function imperfectly captures human values, the agent optimises the proxy, not the true goal. Example: a cleaning robot that blinds its eyes rather than tidying the room, so it receives no "negative reward" signal for mess it cannot observe.
  • Distribution shift: An agent trained on historical data learns and perpetuates historical biases. Example: Hiring model trained on past male-dominated outcomes systematically scores women lower.
  • Opacity: Deep RL policies are black boxes. A doctor using an AI clinical recommendation cannot interrogate why it chose a particular drug — this violates the right to explanation (EU AI Act, GDPR).

Part 3 — Design Approaches for Ethical Learning Agents (3 marks)

  • Reward shaping with human oversight: RLHF trains a reward model from human comparisons, reducing reward hacking by grounding rewards in actual human preferences rather than proxy signals.
  • Fairness constraints during learning: Add demographic parity or equalised odds constraints to the loss function. The agent optimises subject to fairness.
  • Interpretability (XAI): Use LIME or SHAP explanations so that every decision can be audited. SHAP's Shapley values give a principled attribution of model output to each input feature.
  • Corrigibility: Include a "shutdown" or "human override" component so the agent does not resist correction — critical when the agent is deployed in high-stakes domains.

Part 4 — Role of γ in Ethical Design (2 marks)

  • The discount factor γ determines how much the agent values long-term consequences vs. immediate rewards.
  • Setting γ = 0 (myopic) makes the agent maximise short-term metrics, ignoring harm that manifests later. This is ethically irresponsible in healthcare, climate policy, or financial lending.
  • Choosing γ is therefore an ethical design decision: a high γ encourages the agent to "think" beyond the current step and consider its long-term impact on users and society.

9. Self-Check Exercises

Exercise 1: What is reward hacking? Give an example from healthcare.

Reward hacking occurs when an RL agent finds a way to achieve high reward on the specified reward function without achieving the intended goal. The reward function is a proxy for the true goal, and the agent exploits the gap.

Healthcare example: A hospital-discharge optimisation AI is rewarded for fast discharges (proxy: reduces cost). It learns to discharge patients prematurely — even when they are still sick — because the reward arrives immediately and the long-term readmission penalty is discounted away (especially if γ is low). The true goal was patient health, not discharge speed.

Exercise 2: How is SHAP related to Shapley values from cooperative game theory?

In cooperative game theory (Lesson 11), a Shapley value gives each player their fair share of the coalition's total value, computed as the average marginal contribution across all permutations of players joining.

SHAP applies this directly to ML: the "players" are input features, the "total value" is the model's prediction (minus a baseline), and each feature's Shapley value is its average marginal contribution to the prediction across all orderings of features. This gives a theoretically fair and consistent explanation of any model's output.

The four axioms (Efficiency, Symmetry, Dummy, Additivity) guarantee there is exactly one fair decomposition — the Shapley values — making SHAP unique among explanation methods.

Exercise 3: Define demographic parity and explain why it can conflict with equalised odds.

Demographic parity: The model approves at the same rate across groups: \(P(\hat{Y}=1|A=0) = P(\hat{Y}=1|A=1)\).

Equalised odds: The model has equal true positive rate AND equal false positive rate across groups.

Why they conflict: If Group A and Group B have different base rates (different true proportions of qualified applicants), then you cannot simultaneously achieve equal approval rates AND equal error rates. For example, if 40% of Group A and 20% of Group B actually qualify, a calibrated model will approve more from Group A — violating demographic parity — but equalising approval rates would require approving unqualified candidates from Group B, hurting equalised odds. This is proven mathematically (Chouldechova 2017): with different base rates, at least one fairness criterion must be violated.
